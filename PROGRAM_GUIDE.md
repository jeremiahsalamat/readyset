# ReadySet: the program and why it is built this way

ReadySet is a four-day powerlifting program that adapts to how you recover. Everything below is
condensed from Israetel, Hoffmann & Smith, *Scientific Principles of Strength Training* (2020),
with the rules that go beyond the book (RPE stops, openers, the layoff and stall rules) drawn from
common powerlifting coaching practice and checked in a fatigue simulation before they went in.
Page numbers refer to that book. Where the app makes a choice the book leaves open, it says so.

## The shape of a macro (22 weeks, or 19 without the Peak block)

| Block | Weeks | Main lifts | Loads (as % of 1RM, TM = 90%) | Sets per lift per session | Then |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build A, then Build B | 4 + 4 | sets of 7-8 (deadlift: sets of 6, then 5) | 70% → 77% (+~3%/week) | 3 → 4, plus earned sets on the competition lift | 1 deload week each |
| Strength A, then Strength B | 3 + 3 | sets of 5, 4, 3; week 3 opens with one single at ~RPE 8 | 77% → 86% (the single ~92%) | 4 (3 in week 3), plus earned sets | 1 deload week each |
| Peak & Test (optional, off by default) | 2 | triples then doubles | 86% → 90% | 3 | test week: one single at RPE 9 per lift |
| Easy week | 1 (2 after a rough macro) | variations, half sets, half loads | | | the next macro |

The sequence is the book's hypertrophy → basic strength → peaking order (p.281), each phase at
least three weeks (p.284), with the hypertrophy phase at 4:1 accumulation to deload and the
strength phase at 3:1, which are the book's recommended starting mesocycle lengths (pp.338-339).
Two runs of each block per macro, with a different variation set in each run (A and B below), is
how coaches get a full year out of the same structure without the lifts going stale (p.250).
The Peak & Test block is off by default. The book says peaking is "only a good idea if you've got
something to show for it" (p.285); without it the maxes come from your logged sets, and the
Strength block's opener single already gives you a heavy single every six weeks.

Deadlifts use sets of 5-6 in Build rather than 7-8: the book's hypertrophy range runs 6-10 (p.338),
and high-rep deadlifts are where form breaks down and lower-back fatigue piles up out of all
proportion to the stimulus, which is why most coaches keep the pull at the low end of the range.

Intensities live inside the book's zones: hypertrophy 60-75% 1RM for 6-10 reps (p.338), strength
75-90% for 3-6 reps (p.339), peaking 85-95% for 1-3 reps (p.340). No working set goes above 90%
outside the Peak block (the one exception is the Strength block's week-3 opener, one single at
about 92% and RPE 8, the routine max check coaches use instead of a test week), because "any training beyond 90% if the meet is far away" is the book's definition
of going too heavy too often (p.167). The Build block never exceeds 80%, which is where the book
says hypertrophy volume stops being sustainable (p.60).

## The week

| Day | Main lift | Second lift | Accessories |
|---|---|---|---|
| A Squat day | Competition squat | Competition bench | leg curl, lat pulldown, abs (optional) |
| B Bench day | Competition bench | Hinge variation | low row, triceps, biceps (optional) |
| C Deadlift day | Competition deadlift | Squat variation | leg extension, face pull, abs (optional) |
| D Bench variation day | Bench variation | Overhead press | pulldown or row, lateral raise (optional), triceps |

That gives squats twice a week, bench muscles three times with one true overload, and one heavy
deadlift plus a hinge variation. This matches the book's lift-specific frequency guidance: quads at
least twice, bench muscles at least twice with the bench itself overloaded about once, heavy
deadlifts once for most lifters (pp.219-220). Two back-to-back rest days are "noticeably more
effective" (p.145), so Mon/Tue/Thu/Fri is the default. Heavy squat, bench and deadlift never fall
on three consecutive days (p.235). The hinge variation sits on Tuesday, two days before the heavy
pull, rather than the day after it: a lower back that is still fatigued from Thursday's deadlift
should not be loaded again on Friday. Friday is upper body only.

Sessions are sized to about 45-75 minutes of lifting with 3 / 2 / 1.5 minute rests; in the
Strength and Peak blocks the main lift gets 4 minutes, because heavy triples and singles are
limited by the nervous system and short rests there turn strength work into conditioning. The
book gives no rest-period numbers; those are conventional. The 75 minutes is a target for the
work the program requires, not a limit on you: the optional slots (abs, biceps, lateral raise) are
left out of it, so they can add 5-10 minutes on the days you choose to do them, and nothing stops
a session that runs longer. What the program prescribes stays time-efficient: the weekly review
will not add a set, to a main lift or to the accessories, if the required work of any day in the
rest of the block would run past about 75 minutes, and set counts a block inherits at its start are
checked the same way. Because the optional slots no longer count, they never cost a main lift an
earned set. Main lifts come first: when time is short, a lift's earned set takes the accessory
set's place. Today and the Program tab show the required time, with the optional minutes beside it.

### Variations rotate once per block

| Slot | Build A | Build B | Strength A | Strength B | Peak |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Squat variation | paused squat | the other bar position* | paused squat | the other bar position* | competition squat (light) |
| Bench variation | close-grip bench | Larsen press | long-pause bench | Spoto press | competition bench (light) |
| Hinge variation | Romanian deadlift | deficit deadlift | paused deadlift | block pull | competition deadlift (light) |

\* high-bar for a low-bar squatter, low-bar for a high-bar squatter. The easy week uses the other
bar position too. The belt squat stays available as a swap for either squat variation.

The book wants exercises changed "once every mesocycle", not weekly (p.250), with variants
different enough to feel different: grips and stances at least a hand-width apart (p.265). The
close-grip cue in the app says exactly that. The competition lifts stay in every block because the
book accepts that as "quite sound" (p.268); the variation lives in the second slots. Build's squat
variation is a barbell squat rather than the belt squat so that the squat pattern is trained with a
bar twice a week, which is what the book's frequency guidance is about (p.219).

## Weekly volume

| Pattern | Build wk 1 / 2 / 3-4 | Strength wk 1 / 2 / 3 | Ceiling (Build / Strength / Peak) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Squat (comp + barbell variants) | 6 / 7 / 8 | 6 / 7 / 6 | 14 / 12 / 8 |
| Bench (comp + barbell variants) | 9 / 10 / 12 | 8 / 10 / 9 | 18 / 16 / 12 |
| Deadlift (comp + paused/deficit) | A 3 / 4 / 4, B 6 / 7 / 8 | 6 / 7 / 6 | 10 / 9 / 6 |

With the optional slots out of the budget, one lift alone can climb its full three earned sets in
Build (about 11 / 15 / 7, deadlift 11 in Build B) and two in Strength (8 / 11 / 8) before the
75 minutes binds; when several lifts climb at once, the budget for the required work is what stops
them first.
These start low on purpose. The book's advice for anyone coming off a program that buried them is
to "start with a much lower volume than they use, and ease in. Increasing volume when you're
recovered is MUCH easier and safer than decreasing volume when you're already beat up" (p.164). The
weekly review then climbs toward your real maximum recoverable volume, which the book says to find
by adding sets slowly "until you're regularly overreaching in the week before deloads" (p.322).

Earned sets go on the competition lift only, not on its variations: coaches add volume to the
movement they want to move, and spreading extra sets across variants doubles the fatigue for the
same signal. The set counts you tolerated in a block are remembered and become the starting point
the next time that block runs, and each run may search one step higher than the last (p.322). The
ceilings are per phase, lowest in Peak, so that no amount of good weeks can rebuild the volume that
wore you down.

On top of the per-lift counts sits the **volume dial**, a lasting offset of −2 to +2 sets on every
barbell exercise (−1 is about 2 squat, 3 bench and 1 deadlift sets a week) that the Program tab shows and lets you set by hand. It steps down (once per block at most)
when the chronic-fatigue signals below fire, and climbs back one step after a clean Build block. Above
zero it is by hand only (a +1 dial adds about 6-7 minutes of required work a session), and it never reaches
the Peak. At −2 the Training Max percentage comes down instead (2.5 points at a time, to 85%). Because the high-volume Build
block is where the dial is lost, only a clean Build block earns a step back below zero (a
low-volume Strength block is clean almost every time and would hand it back too early). A
lowered Training Max comes back first: each clean block restores 2.5 points, up to the percentage
you chose. This is what turns "this lifter
cannot recover from the written volume" into a program change rather than a repeated deload.

## How it adapts

### Before a session: the check-in (6 taps)

Sleep, energy, motivation, and soreness by region, combined into a 0-100 readiness score.

| Score | What happens | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 50+ green | As planned. The first set of the main lift may take bonus reps: up to +2 in Build, +1 in Strength, none in Peak, and never past RPE 9. Deadlift takes +1 at most, with a rep held back | Rep PRs are "the most common" powerlifting PRs (p.86); failure is avoided (p.97) |
| 35-49 yellow | The session stands, trimmed: loads 2.5% down, no bonus reps, no earned extra sets. Same reps, same planned sets | "Most of those times, an overload is quite possible" (p.96); a small load nudge keeps the overload without the grind |
| under 35 red | Light session recommended (Build: half the sets at 90% load; Strength: 70% / 70%; Peak: half the sets at 80%), or rest and keep the session for next time | The book's light-session recipe (pp.148-149). Light beats a day off for both fatigue and fitness (p.179) |

A second red check-in in one week switches the rest of that week to light sessions. That is the
book's rule for a week that goes wrong: "Use Extra Light Sessions, not Deloads" (p.160).

All "OK" answers with slightly sore legs score 53 and count as a normal day. The 50 line is a design
choice, as 55-60 was: no validated cut-off exists, and the evidence on subjective wellness supports
watching the trend rather than any single number (Saw et al. 2016).

**Coming back after a gap.** If a week or more passes without a session, the Today tab offers the
layoff rule before you train: 7-13 days off, the current week repeats 5% lighter with earned sets
cleared; 14-20 days, the block restarts at week 1 with the maxes 2.5% lower and the first week 2.5%
lighter; 21-41 days, the block restarts at week 1 (from Peak, the last Strength block) with the maxes
5% lower and the first week 5% lighter; six weeks or more, the macro restarts at Build week 1 with
the maxes 10% lower (sets this easy read them back slowly, so raise them by hand if the weights feel
light). The week's cut applies to barbell loads, the opener single included. Strength is
generally maintained for up to about four weeks without training (Mujika & Padilla 2001; Bosquet et
al. 2013), so the shorter restarts stay small; the 42-day line is a judgment call. If the gap grows
past a tier you already answered, the tougher rule is offered again, and it applies only what is left
of its cut. Resuming at the old loads after
a layoff is how people get hurt, and the book is explicit that fitness fades faster than the numbers
in your head (p.250). You can decline it. Skipped days count as time off, not training. A deload or
easy week is light enough to absorb a short gap, but two weeks or more off is offered the rule there
too.

### Warm-ups

Every barbell lift starts with warm-up sets built from the first working weight, not from the 1RM:
then steps at roughly 40%, 55%, 70%, 80% and 90% of the working weight, with reps falling from 8
to a single as the load rises. Jumps shrink near the top and the last step is a single at about
90%, so the first work set never arrives as a surprise. Working weights of 300 lb and up take
fewer reps per step (40% × 5, 55% × 3, 70% × 2, 80% × 1, 90% × 1), and 450 lb and up one step
more (30% × 5, 45% × 5, 60% × 3, 72% × 2, 82% × 1, 90% × 1), which gets a heavy squat or
deadlift warm without spending the session on warm-ups; the second barbell lift of a session,
already warm, gets two or three steps. Steps closer than 10 lb to the one before are left out. In Strength week 3 the main lift adds an **opener**: one
single at about 92% of your 1RM (RPE 8, two clean reps in reserve) after the warm-ups and before
the triples. It is the single most useful piece of information the app gets about your real max,
it practises the thing powerlifting is actually about, and it is not a test: set the safeties and
stop at one. Rest is a
minute between warm-ups and up to two before the first work set. The book gives no warm-up
protocol; this is the common strength-coaching practice, and it exists because a first set that
feels heavy after a lazy warm-up is the most common reason a good day gets logged as a bad one.
Warm-up sets never count toward volume, reviews or max estimates. They can be hidden in Settings.

### During a session: every logged set re-aims the rest of the exercise

Each done working set with an RPE (a missed set with no RPE counts as RPE 10 at the reps you got;
bonus reps count as reps) implies a 1RM through the rep-max table. The app combines the evidence so far and
sets the remaining sets to the weight that would land the planned reps at the target RPE, from the
same table. So RPE 8 on a set planned at RPE 5 is three reps closer to failure than intended, and
the next set drops by what the table says (about 9%), not by a fixed step.

**Missed reps or stopped short.** A set with fewer reps than planned counts as *missed reps* only
when you rate it RPE 9 or more or leave it unrated (then it reads as RPE 10 at the reps you got),
or when it did not go up at all (0 reps, at any rating). Rated RPE 8.5 or below, it *stopped short*
with reps left: it is not a failure, and it counts like any set through its reps and RPE (5 reps at
RPE 7 reads as a set of 8 to failure). The same rule holds everywhere below: the first-set weight,
the fatigue stop, the floor, calibration, the History detail and the weekly review.

- The first working set counts half: it often feels heaviest for reasons that are not fitness.
  One hard first set therefore moves the next set about half way; a second hard set confirms it
  and the full correction lands. A first set that was clearly hard (1.5+ RPE over target, or reps
  missed) counts in full: that is not a warm-up effect.
- The opener single counts in full, read against its own target of RPE 8.
- Sets logged at RPE 6-6.5 count half, and RPE 4-5.5 about a third: the further from failure, the
  less precise anyone's rating is. They still move the remaining sets, within the usual limits, so an
  easy day that is easier than planned nudges the load up and one that is harder brings it down.
- It re-runs from scratch on any edit: tick, untick, an RPE changed later, a rep count or weight
  corrected. Picking an RPE on a set you have not ticked yet ticks it.
- A weight you type into an undone set yourself is left alone.
- Limits per exercise: never below 85% or above 102.5% of the planned weight (100% in Peak and in
  light sessions, which exist to cut fatigue).
- **The fatigue stop.** The RTS-style rule most coaches use: missed reps (not a set stopped short), a set that is still two
  or more RPE over target (and at least RPE 8.5, so an easy day planned at RPE 5 is not cut short by
  an RPE 7 set) after the weight has already been corrected, or the floor being reached, ends the
  exercise. The remaining sets are cut automatically, with a Restore button if you know
  better. Grinding on past that point costs more recovery than it buys, and it is exactly the kind
  of session that used to bury you.
- Deload, easy and test weeks are exempt; their loads are light on purpose.
- Changing a max in Settings during a session rebases every undone set on the new Training Max
  (and a plate or bar change re-rounds them to what the plates make). A rebase never cuts sets on
  its own: the fatigue stop waits for the next set you log.

### After a session: the maxes calibrate themselves

The number you typed at setup is only a starting point. After each training session the app
reads every working set with an RPE on a competition lift (or a close barbell variation such as
close-grip bench or paused squat), turns each into an implied 1RM, and takes a confidence-weighted
average: variations half weight when mixed with competition sets, first sets half weight, RPE 6-6.5
half weight. Rating every set 1 RPE too high ends up with loads about 3% lighter than they could be.

- If today's estimate is more than 3% under the current 1RM, the 1RM drops to it at once, at most
  5% per session. Fatigue management is the priority, so bad news is acted on immediately. The one
  guard: sets far from failure (reported and planned under RPE 7) are vague evidence, so they can
  only lower a max when the shortfall is 5% or more, or when reps were missed (a set stopped short at
  RPE 8.5 or below is not a miss: it neither blocks a rise nor opens the 15% floor below). In the program's very
  first week a drop is the number you entered being corrected, not fatigue: it does not count toward
  the "max lowered twice" signal, and the 6% test starts over from the new max.
- The 1RM rises only on a smoothed estimate (half today, half history), by at most 2.5% per
  session (on the 2.5 lb grid, rounded so it never passes the cap; a light max can still move one step) and at most once per week per lift, never after a session rated "crushed me", never on
  one set's evidence, and a fast rise (today's sets alone more than 4% over) needs at least one set
  near failure.
- Sets under RPE 6.5 cannot say how much was left, but they still prove a floor: read as if each
  were RPE 6.5, two sessions in a row that clear the max by 4% raise it (2.5% at most, once a
  week), so a max entered too low climbs back. When even that cannot prove more (a max set far too
  low leaves every set at RPE 4-5), three such sessions in a row bring a plain note suggesting you
  raise it in Settings.
- Late in a block, sets are expected to feel heavier than they are (accumulated fatigue, p.117),
  so the estimate that can raise a max is read up to 3% higher in the last accumulation week; the
  estimate that can lower it is not. Calibration alone can never take more than 10% off a max in
  one block (15% if reps were missed).
- The Training Max follows, so the next session is already right-sized. The weekly review then
  only manages volume (sets); load is handled here.
- Deload, easy and test weeks don't calibrate. The test week sets the 1RM directly from your
  heaviest single with an RPE (the best of several noisy readings runs high), within 5% down and 12%
  up. A single with no RPE can raise the max but not lower it, and a single that did not go up caps
  it just under that weight. The new max is never below a single you made, even above +12%: a test
  single you ticked with at least one rep, at any RPE (10 included) or none, is a floor, and the max
  is then exactly that weight, on the 2.5 lb grid or not (rounding never takes it under). Everything
  above the made weight still follows the caps: a single at 370 rated RPE 7 on a 340 max reads as
  more than 380 but gives 380 (+12%); a single of 400 made on the same max gives 400.
- A session you log after the fact is read like any other. One you edit or delete, if it belongs to
  the current block, puts the maxes back to where the block started and re-reads every session of
  the block in date order, so the correction lands exactly as if it had been logged that way. A past
  session dated before a max you set by hand does not move that max.

The History tab shows the weekly average of those implied maxes per lift against the current
1RM, which is the honest trend: it moves with how you are lifting and feeling, not with what you
entered at setup. Each grey dot is one session, bigger when its sets carry more weight. Tap any
dot to see that session: every set with the max it implies and how much it counted (and why), the
sets left out, that week's average, and what the calibration did with the 1RM afterwards.
Calibration can be switched off in Settings; a max you set by hand is respected until your sets
say otherwise.

### Your data

On an iPhone the home-screen app keeps its own storage, separate from Safari, and iOS deletes it
when the icon is deleted. The app therefore offers a backup file (share sheet to Files, iCloud
Drive or AirDrop) and a clipboard copy. Backing up is your decision: Settings can offer a one-tap
backup card after each session, and shows how many sessions are newer than your last backup.
Restore from the file picker on the setup screen or in Settings.

Forgot to log a session, or logged it wrong? **Log a past session** on the Today tab builds the
next planned session on the date you choose (no check-in, no timer) and then runs it through the
same finish, calibration and weekly review as a live one. **Edit** on any session in History
reopens it in the logger: retype sets, tick or untick, change the date, the bike minutes or how it
felt, then save. Both are undoable, and both feed the maxes and the next review like any other
session; set counts from a weekly review that already ran are left as they were.

### Rest timer, notifications and closing the app

Ticking a main-lift set logs it at once and starts its rest; then the app asks how hard it was
(skip it if you did not notice). The rest timer is stored, so closing the app mid-rest and
reopening shows the right remaining time, or how long ago it ended. Every set entry saves as you
type, and a session in progress reopens at the set you are on.

What an iPhone allows a web app: it runs while it is on screen and for a few seconds after you
switch away, then iOS freezes it until you come back. So with ReadySet open (the screen stays on
during a session) every rest ends with a chime and a flash; once you switch to another app or lock
the phone, no web app can alert you on time, ReadySet included. The timer bar shows the clock time
each rest ends, and when you come back it shows how long ago it ended (Rest done +1:10). A
notification can be switched on in Settings, but it only reaches you while ReadySet is open or in
those first seconds after switching away.

### Typos

A number that is probably a slip of the thumb (36 for 360, a weight under the empty bar, 18 reps for
8, a test single 15% over your max) is asked about before it can move the next sets or the maxes:
use the planned number (today's corrected one, when the sets were re-aimed), keep yours, or fix it. A weight you keep carries to the later sets without
asking again. Calibration also leaves out a set lifted far from
its planned weight (under 60% or over 140% of it) unless you kept that number at the check, and
says so. A fatigue stop that was triggered by a number you then correct undoes itself.

### After a session: one tap

Easy / Solid / Very hard / Crushed me. Together with the RPE you log on each set and the soreness
you report at the next check-in, this feeds the weekly review.

### Every four sessions: the weekly review

For each competition lift the app scores the week:

- Missed reps or RPE 1.5+ above plan: −2. RPE 0.75+ above plan: −1. Below plan: +1. Bonus reps
  are taken back out before comparing, since they raise the RPE by design; a set stopped short is
  compared as if the missing reps had been done (its RPE plus the reps short, at most 10), so 5 of 8
  at RPE 7 against a plan of RPE 8 reads as RPE 10, never as easier than planned. In week 1 of a block an
  RPE above plan scores nothing: calibration has already answered it, and one week cannot show
  accumulated fatigue. Missed reps and a bad session rating still count.
- Main sets on target: +1. Session rated easy: +1. Very hard: −1. Crushed: −2.
- Fully recovered by the next session: +1. Still sore: −1. Very sore: −2.
- Missed reps on a second lift: −1.

On target alone holds. Score +2 or better adds a set next week on the competition lift (until the run's ceiling, the
phase cap, or the 75-minute budget for the required work; with calibration off, a very easy week at the ceiling
earns a bigger load jump instead). Sets logged without an RPE give no read on effort, so they never
count as "on target" and never earn a set. When on target plus recovered is all the good news, a set is added at most every second week,
so a lifter who simply hits the numbers climbs slowly rather than in a straight line (p.164).
Score −2 or worse removes a set from the lift and each close variation and raises a flag; with calibration off it also takes 2.5% off next
week's loads (−5% at −4).
Nothing is added in the last accumulation week, which the book expects to be the overreaching week
before the deload (p.117), and the first week after a deload earns nothing for feeling fresh.

**Acute signals** make the app suggest taking the deload now: two flags each on two lifts, two red
check-ins, or two crushing sessions in one week. Days switched to light by the two-red rule
count as that one bad week, not as chronic fatigue. **Chronic signals** say the written volume itself
is too much for you, and they also turn the volume dial down (once per block): four red check-ins
or four light sessions in a block, a lift's max lowered twice (or by 6%) within a block, or
readiness that has sat under 50 for two weeks running (one week under 40). A single chronic signal in the last accumulation week does not move the
dial, because that week is supposed to feel like too much, though it does cost the block its clean
status. Readiness alone can step the dial down but never lowers the Training Max.

Accepting the suggestion (or "Deload now" on the Program tab) inserts a deload week and the block
resumes at the week it was on; in the last accumulation week it runs the block's own deload
instead, so you never get deload, overreach, deload in a row. A suggestion you neither take nor
decline lapses at the end of the week, and the next review raises it again if it still applies. "Push on" keeps the block going but trims the coming week: no bonus
reps, no earned sets, loads 2.5% down. The signals come straight from the book's MRV
indicators: not matching last week's reps (p.126, pp.338-339), the bar feeling disproportionately
heavy, low desire to train, poor sleep (p.77), being dull and tired instead of sore (p.125).

### The end of a macro: the stall rule

When a macro ends, each lift's smoothed estimate is compared with the end of the previous macro.
One macro under 1% is inside the noise of the reading, so it takes two in a row. Then a lift that was
never flagged twice was under-stimulated: its Build runs start one set higher, if a session has room
and the dial did not step down that macro. A lift that was flagged was under-recovered: it starts one
set lower and the easy week stretches to two. That is the book's "if volume isn't working, change the
volume" test (p.322), run on your real numbers, so that a silent stall is caught after two macros
rather than left to drift.

### Deload weeks

First half of the week: half the sets at 90% of the overload load (Build) or 70% / 70% (Strength).
Second half: half the sets at half the load. That is the book's deload table (pp.152-153). The book
is blunt that "if your deload is tough, you're not dropping the fatigue you should be" (p.174).
Sessions in a deload week never count toward reviews or max calibration.

### Training Max

Every barbell load is a percentage of a Training Max, 90% of your 1RM by default, so a session is
always doable on a mediocre day. With calibration on (the default) the 1RM moves after every
session as described above, and the end of a block changes nothing further. With calibration off,
the old block-end rules apply: a higher estimate from logged sets raises the 1RM up to 5% per block,
a clean block (every planned rep done, so a set stopped short is not clean either) earns +5 lb
(squat, deadlift) or +2.5 lb (bench), a lift flagged twice drops 2.5%.
Either way the Peak block's test single sets the 1RM directly.

### Accessories

Self-loaded work (belt squat, dumbbells, machines) uses double progression: when every set reaches
the top of the rep range, the weight goes up next time (one dumbbell step, or 5 lb); a set that falls
short of the bottom of the range brings it down by the same step. Accessory sets sit at 2-4 per exercise and
only climb when no lift is flagged. The book caps isolation work at about 30% of a hypertrophy
program and wants little or none in strength phases (pp.338-339); here it runs about 26-31% with the
optional slots dropped. Biceps, lateral raises and abs are marked optional so they are the first
thing to drop on a short day.

## Cardio

The BikeErg block after lifting is for health and recovery, not training stress. The book's
guidance on cardio for powerlifters: keep it low impact (biking is fine, running is not), keep it
under roughly 1,500 kcal per week, and judge the dose by whether the lifts keep improving rather
than by how it feels (pp.351-353). That 1,500 kcal is a ceiling, not a target: 100-150 minutes a
week of genuinely easy cycling covers the health and recovery benefit, and more mostly adds
fatigue that the lifts then pay for. The app's default is 40 minutes after each session (about
1,050 kcal a week at an easy 110 W) and it shows the weekly running estimate against the ceiling.

- 30-45 minutes after lifting at a pace where nose breathing is comfortable is a sensible default.
- If you want 60-90 minutes some days, keep it at the very easy end and watch the weekly total.
- A light session day halves the cardio target.
- Off-day walking or easy biking can be logged in Settings and counts toward the weekly total.

## Eating for it

The program cannot see your food, but it assumes the book's advice: a small surplus during Build
(muscle is what that block is for, and it does not come out of a deficit), maintenance in Strength
and Peak (pp.338-340). A hard cut in Build will read as chronic fatigue and the app will take volume
away, which is the right response to the signals and the wrong outcome for the block.

## What the app deliberately does not do

- No sets to failure and no AMRAP sets; bonus reps are capped (p.97, p.133).
- No training above 90% outside the Peak block (p.167).
- No week-to-week exercise rotation (p.273).
- No "lighter weight, more reps" deloads; both volume and intensity drop (pp.173-174).
- No block-to-block drift of focus: each block keeps one rep zone, though reps undulate inside it
  (sets of 5, 4 and 3 across a Strength block; 6s and 5s for the deadlift in Build), which the
  book endorses (pp.41-42, 304-305).
- No unplanned deloads from a single bad day; a bad day becomes a light session (p.160).

## Honest limits

- RPE is a skill. For the first few weeks, log it conservatively; the review tolerates noise but
  consistent over- or under-reporting will push the volume the wrong way.
- 1RM estimates from sets of 7-8 are rough. The Strength block's opener single, and the optional
  Peak block's test single, are the real numbers.
- The rules beyond the book were tuned in a fitness-fatigue simulation of four lifter types, not
  on you. It tests whether the rules behave, not how fast you will gain: expect low single digits
  a year at best once past the intermediate stage. Your own log is the only evidence that counts.
- The readiness score weights are a design choice (sleep 30%, energy 25%, soreness 25%,
  motivation 20%); the book names these signals but gives no formula.
- This is a program, not a coach. Pain, illness, and life stress still need your own judgment.
