## Chapter 7 — Variation (pp. 243–277)

### Definition and mechanisms
- Variation = "The manipulation of training variables to prevent staleness and injury and to magnify the long-term adaptive response to training." [243]
- Variables that can be varied: volume range, intensity range, rep range/metabolite load, exercise selection, frequency, velocity (each low/moderate/high). Exercise order changes make a "likely tiny difference" and are ignored. [243–244]
- Mechanism: negative feedback loops → "adaptive resistance": "The more we signal a system to adapt in a particular direction, the more resistance to adaptation it develops." Fix = stop stressing the system one way, stress it a similar-but-different way, then return later when resistance has fallen. [246–247]
- Four systems that develop adaptive resistance: technique, hypertrophy, neurally-mediated strength, connective tissue. [247]

### Technique
- Three phases of learning a technique: slow early phase, rapid phase, then slow/stagnant/regressing phase caused by adaptive resistance. [247–248]
- If competition bench "feels off" and isn't improving week to week technically: "stop competition pressing altogether for several weeks" and use wide/close grip, incline or dumbbell work; reintroduction allows re-learning with better cues and fewer old mistakes. [248]
- "regular 'deletion and replacement' of techniques may yield best results." [248]

### Hypertrophy
- Each exercise overloads some fibers heavily while others only get maintenance stimulus; the heavily loaded fibers (a) locally overreach past MRV and (b) develop adaptive resistance within weeks. Changing exercises resolves both. Higher DOMS on a new exercise signals the re-sensitisation. [249–250]
- **Rotate exercises once every mesocycle**; "too often would interfere with the Specificity sub-principle of Directed Adaptation." This works "for several mesocycles on end" but not infinitely. [250]
- After "multiple months" of hypertrophy training, mTOR signalling blunts and AMPk dominates; a "wholesale declination from hypertrophy training" (i.e. a strength phase) re-sensitises it. Strength training is "almost the perfect break from hypertrophic training" because it conserves muscle but lacks the volume/high reps/short rests that generate metabolites. [250]

### Neural strength
- "Months upon months" of maximal, low-rep work becomes less stimulative even with good fatigue management. Remedy: "a mesocycle or two" of repetitive sub-maximal work (hypertrophy / work-capacity training) — "The stimulus to the nervous system just has to be significantly different, not less." [250–252]

### Connective tissue
- Novel force lines adapt quickly, then slow; changing exercise (e.g. squat → front squat) re-ignites adaptation on return. No variation → same micro-tears get aggravated → higher injury rate. Variation lets you "train with overloads for longer but still reaping the benefits of reduced injury rates." [252]

### General pattern and limits
- "changing those variables [exercise, volume, intensity] strategically every mesocycle or so offers the best tradeoffs of variation with directed adaptation." [253]
- Two limits: (1) too-frequent variation ("trying a new exercise every week") prevents adaptations settling; (2) variants must stay within powerlifting specificity — hypertrophy variants must build muscle well, strength variants must build strength well. [253]
- Technical definition: "the strategic alteration of training variables to decrease adaptive resistance, bounded by the specificity of the demands of powerlifting training." [254]
- "If it gets you big, strong, or peaked for training, then in its right place it's probably just a fine variant to use." [254]

### Proper timing of variation
- Remove a competition lift and replace it with a variant "for at least a mesocycle"; on re-introduction improvement is faster. [256]
- Constraints on when: (1) Specificity — competition lifts must be used "at least in the mesocycle leading up to the meet, if not for several mesocycles before"; (2) Fatigue management — peaking volumes are low so there's no room for extra variants; "we cannot use much if any variation in the mesocycle or two leading up to the meet"; (3) intensity must be heaviest right before the meet, so light/high-rep variation can't go there either. [256–257]
- Therefore the best time for exercise + intensity variation is the "deep offseason (aka general prep phases)", right after the last meet and far from the next. [257]
- Macro pattern: with each mesocycle from one competition to the next, specificity rises and variation falls. Exercises get biomechanically closer to the comp lifts (skull crushers → close grip bench → competition bench); average intensity rises each mesocycle "until it approaches competition weights". The transition is gradual, no hard cutoff. [257–258]
- Intensity variation: "there is not much leeway in intensity anyway once the three different phases are applied properly" — it is handled by Phase Potentiation. [259]

### Strategic (directed) variation: which variants to choose
- Two selection rules: Adaptive Proclivity Training (train your genetic strengths) and Limiting Factor Training (train true weak points). [259]
- Adaptive Proclivity: "works especially well for intermediate lifters." After "several macrocycles of non-directed variation" (training every PL muscle evenly, picking effective lifts that are not the powerlifts themselves) you can see which lifts/body parts gain fastest; then choose early-macrocycle variants to make those stronger and bigger. Examples: responsive quads → leg press, front squat; big triceps → close grip and triceps work; responsive upper back → emphasise it in deadlift work. Do this "for a long time (years) after your beginner phase is over." [259–261]
- Limiting Factor: applies once a strong base exposes a true weak point (quads too weak out of the hole; triceps can't lock out what the chest launches; back rounds while grip/hams are fine). Variants early in the macrocycle target them: "Pause squats and front squats for quads, wide grip benches and dumbbell flyes for the chest, and rowing, pull-ups, and block pulling for the upper back." Works "especially well for intermediate and advanced lifters." [261–262]
- Beginner/early-intermediates who are "weak all over" don't have limiting factors; they should still train strong points preferentially because "your strong points will respond better to training." [262]
- Long-term pattern for advanced lifters: alternate phases on advantages with phases on weak points; never stop training advantages. "Play to your strengths, so long as your weaknesses don't hold you back." [263–264]
- Once past beginner, don't do the same variants as your training partner. [264]

### How much variation is enough (minimum difference between variants)
- Variants must "feel different" (different neural/muscular recruitment). [264–265]
- Squat bar position: only 3 categories count as distinct variants — front, high bar, low bar. "Medium bar" / in-between variants do not. [265]
- Grip widths and stances: at least one hand-width/foot-width apart; "around 3-4 inches apart from one another" for bench, squat and deadlift. Pinkies one inch in from the rings is NOT a close-grip bench. [265]

### Under-application
- Same sets/reps/intensity all year (e.g. pure 5×5 forever): well suited to basic strength, "poorly suited to both peaking and hypertrophy"; doesn't accommodate progression or individual MRV (some need more volume over mesocycles; for others 5×5 is too much from the start). "A good powerlifting program will usually have a wide range of sets, reps and intensities based on the phase of training." [265–266]
- Doing only the competition lifts all year: loses directed adaptation (no way to target e.g. triceps) and never reduces adaptive resistance. Ed Coan reportedly used close variants (shoulder press, high bar squat, deficit pulls) as the bulk of early-macro training. [266–268]
- Never removing the competition lifts (always doing them alongside variants): "quite sound" but improvable — the comp lifts' adaptive resistance is never reduced. Far from a meet, all hypertrophy work can be done with variants, while the comp lifts "regain their sensitivity." [268–269]

### Over-application
- Four constraints on directed variation: non-specific variants, non-overloading variants, phase-inappropriate variants, too-frequent variation. [270]
- Non-specific: running, sets of 20–30 reps, over-use of calf raises/curls. [270–271]
- Non-overloading: band rear laterals, leg extensions, reverse curls, one-arm cable flyes are low-overload; prefer heavily loadable moves (DB flyes with 100s × 8 strict, front squat). "don't overuse such low-overload moves." [271–272]
- Phase-inappropriate: low bar squat is a poor quad-hypertrophy tool (back/glutes/hams tire first); DB flyes for sets of 5 as a "strength" move risks pec tears; banded cambered-bar squats in peaking = peaking the wrong lift. Rule: "stick to more focused lifts that are conducive to high reps when hypertrophy is the goal, stick only to stable compound lifts and away from isolation moves during strength phases, and focus most of your energy on the actual competition moves during peaking training." [272]
- Too-frequent exercise rotation (Westside-style weekly rotation) violates directed adaptation; PRs on variants "failed to translate to steady strength gains." Triceps mass from close grip "has more of a chance of sticking around" if concentrated in a dedicated mesocycle. [273–274]
- Rep-range rotation (DUP): within-week variation that stays in one physiological range (e.g. 6s / 4s / 2s all in the strength range) is "an effective way to provide variation and manage fatigue at the same time"; "A well designed powerlifting program is likely to have some variation in rep range within the week." Extreme DUP (12s / 6s / 2s in one week) violates directed adaptation and modality compatibility. [274–276]
- Interference list: hypertrophy needs high volume and raises fatigue; strength needs lower fatigue and forceful low-endurance neural traits; peaking needs the lowest volume and fatigue, fast-twitch conversion and explosive neural traits; strength training's fatigue is still too high for peaking. [275]

### Key points
- "Training cannot be spontaneous and random for best results; rather training should be focused for periods of time and subtly varied between training phases." [277]

## Chapter 8 — Phase Potentiation (pp. 278–307)

### Definition and logic
- "the logical sequencing of training phases to promote the best overall long-term outcomes." A phase can potentiate the next (muscle built now makes strength training more productive later). [278–279]
- Three assumptions: Specificity (can't train everything maximally at once; "If you do sets of 6 and 10 in the same workout, the world does not come to an end"), Sequence, Adaptive Decay. [279–280]
- Sequence: peaking must be last; size training partially reverses strength's neural/tissue-alignment adaptations, so strength must precede peaking and size must come first → "build the size, make it stronger, peak for performance." [281]

### Adaptive decay facts (the numbers that set phase lengths)
- a) "Muscle size can be conserved indefinitely with only strength and no hypertrophy training." [284]
- b) Peaking training conserves muscle size for 1–3 months before declines affect strength: ~1 month for beginners, ~3 months for advanced. [284]
- c) Hypertrophy phases longer than about 3–6 months benefit from the lower, re-sensitising volumes of a strength phase. [284]
- d) Within 3–6 months most neural/tissue-alignment strength adaptations have been made; further progress without returning to hypertrophy is "needlessly slow", unless it's time to peak. [284–285]
- e) "Switching phases more often than every 3 weeks or so violates directed adaptation." [284]

### Emergent structure
- Hypertrophy phase: 3 weeks to 6 months. Strength phase: 3 weeks to 6 months. Peaking phase: 3 weeks to 3 months. [285]
- Competition frequency spectrum: some can compete as often as every 9 weeks; others do 6 mo hypertrophy + 6 mo strength + 3 mo peaking and compete every ~1.5 years. [285]
- **No-meet option (directly relevant):** a lifter "might forego competition for longer" and "string together hypertrophy phases with strength phases ... for several 6 week to one-year macrocycles." "Peaking for powerlifting is only a good idea if you've got something to show for it... there is nothing wrong at all with repeating that paradigm once, twice, or as many times as you choose." [285]
- Blended phases: a small amount of basic strength work at the tail end of a hypertrophy phase eases the transition; a small amount of extra volume in a strength phase allows a little more growth; extra strength work early in peaking lets peaking run longer. Limit: **≥70% phase-specific work, ≤30% non-phasic work** at any time, or you won't benefit maximally. [285–286]

### Importance rank
- Phase potentiation is "the second-least important principle" — net advantage "on the order of several percent." "Add volume to get bigger, take it away to get stronger, and do the right thing to peak." Matters most for advanced lifters counting single-digit % gains per year. [286–287]

### Phase prescriptions (Chapter 8 list) [289–291]
| Block | Adaptation goal | Preservation goal | Modalities | Overload sessions / muscle / wk | Sets / muscle / wk | Reps | Avg intensity | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hypertrophy / work capacity | Muscle size + work capacity for later strength | Don't let fibre type/neural traits drift from strength — "sets far over 10 reps may not be recommended" | Compound variations, limited isolation; focus on muscles that need size (e.g. pecs for bench) | 2–6 | 15–30 overloading | 6–10 | 60–75% 1RM | 3 wk–6 mo; usually 2–3 months for intermediates |
| Basic strength | General strength | Maintain size | Compound variations, limited or no isolation; focus on movements that need strength (e.g. SLDL for posterior chain) | 2–4 | 10–20 overloading | 3–6 | 75–90% 1RM | 3 wk–6 mo; usually 3–4 months for intermediates |
| Peaking | Technical + neural ability at 1RM loads | Maintain size and basic strength | Mostly the competition lifts, little variation; light sessions likely | 1–3 (+ light sessions) | 5–10 overloading | 1–3 | ~75% average, split between light sessions at 50% and overload sessions at 85–95% | 3 wk–3 mo; usually 1–2 months for intermediates |

### Conserving progress between phases
- "Preserving established gains is much easier than making new ones." But newly built weak/hard-gaining areas need more than usual even to maintain: carry a transitional exercise forward (e.g. quads built with leg press/front squat in hypertrophy → keep extra front/high-bar squat volume in strength → keep a bit more high-bar in peaking; triceps built with skull crushers → favour close-grip work in the first strength meso rather than switching to wide-grip/incline). After a full macrocycle of focus, those gains are decay-resistant and need less attention. [291–292]

### Tapering & peaking (meet-specific; recorded for completeness)
- Taper goals: maintain fitness, drop fatigue; preparedness = fitness + fatigue. [292]
- An intentional overreach "within several weeks of the meet" can yield a net benefit (start of this discussion on p. 293 is missing from the extraction). [294]
- Three taper parts: Part 1 normal peaking volume OR functional overreach at 1.5–2× normal peaking volume; Part 2 volume reduced to 90–50% of normal peaking, intensity maintained or raised; Part 3 ~50% volume and 50% intensity of normal peaking. Optional extra rest days after Part 3, then meet. [294, 298, 341]
- Taper length drivers: lifter size, strength, experience (bigger/stronger/more experienced → longer taper; experienced lifters are decay-resistant and tolerate it). "You can't just scale up a program or a taper based on percentages." [295–296]
- Category calculator: bodyweight <165 lb / 165–220 / >220 = 1/2/3 pts; total Class 2 or lower / Class 1 or Master / Elite-Pro = 1/2/3; experience <3 yr / 3–6 / >6 = 1/2/3. Sum 3–4 → Cat 1; 5–7 → Cat 2; 8–9 → Cat 3. [297–298, 341–342]
- Cat 1: 1-week taper (Part 1: 0–3 d, Part 2: 1–4 d, Part 3: 0–3 d). Cat 2: 2 weeks (4–5 d each part). Cat 3: 3 weeks (1 wk each part). [298–299, 342]

### Under-application (phases too long)
- Hypertrophy: chronic high volume desensitises growth regulators; "once every several months, low volume training must be employed for at least a mesocycle." Unlikely to keep working past ~6 months; authors' advice: at "3 months or so" insert at least a month-long lower-volume mesocycle or move into the strength block. [300–301]
- Strength: limit exclusive strength training to ≤6 months, "perhaps closer to 3 months for most lifters"; after several months on non-increasing muscle mass, neural/architectural changes are maxed. Then either run another hypertrophy phase or peak. Long "strength-only" gains stories usually involve weight/muscle gain, i.e. accidental hypertrophy — better to do it deliberately and only eat hypercalorically during high-volume training. [301–302]
- Peaking: >3 months risks muscle and strength; "most lifters should err on the side of 2 months maximum." Heavy 1–3s don't even provide enough volume for strength gains in intermediates/advanced; technical adaptations run their course "after only weeks." [302]

### Over-application
- All abilities in one session (e.g. 3×3 @90% + 5×5 @80% + 4×10 @65% per lift): ~3-hour sessions, and the hypertrophy work reverses the neural adaptations "literally within the next hour." [303]
- All abilities each week (extreme DUP): directed adaptation violated; heavy triples within days of high-volume work may not even be an overload. [304–305]
- Phases shorter than ~3 weeks: "needlessly complicates the training process." "Why stop growing muscle if muscle growth is still going strong?" [305]

### Summary / key points
- "Put on muscle over several months; make the new muscle stronger over several months; take a month or two to peak ... and repeat!" [306]
- "Peaking for a meet will not result in meaningful muscle atrophy (assuming normal training is resumed shortly after)." [307]

## Chapter 9 — Individual Differences (pp. 308–336)

### Framework
- Inter-individual (genetics, sleep, nutrition, drugs, training age) vs intra-individual (same person at different times; training age is the biggest source). Every program "should ideally have room for some sort of alterations." [308–309]
- Individual differences are confined to 5 areas: MRV; fatigue & fitness decay times; development status/goals; exercise selection; exercise technique. [309–310]

### 1. MRV
- Fast-twitch (talented) lifters tend to have *lower* MRVs and overreach when matching set-for-set with less talented partners. [310]
- MRV rises over time with volume phases, better diet/sleep/program design; "What used to be a very effective dose of training can later be barely enough to maintain performance." [310]

### 2. Fatigue and fitness decay times
- Bigger, stronger, more experienced → slower fatigue dissipation and slower fitness decay; genetics can split these. [311]
- Slow fatigue drop + fast fitness decay → more frequent training and a less aggressive taper. [311]
- Taper matrix: slow fatigue drop + slow fitness decay → longer, more aggressive tapers; both quick → shorter, less aggressive; slow fatigue/fast fitness → long tapers that don't cut volume drastically; fast fatigue/slow fitness → "hardly need to taper at all, with perhaps nothing more than a deload." Find yours via coach, measurement (bar velocity, force plate) or trial and error. [312]

### 3. Development status
- Beginner (1–3 yr): mostly hypertrophy; longer hypertrophy phases, shorter strength/peaking phases, shorter active rest; train all movements/muscles evenly. [312–313]
- Intermediate (3–6 yr): still full hypertrophy phases, plus can "maximally benefit from extended (3-6 month) strength phases"; don't need long peaks; need average-length active rest; focus on proclivities (quads for close-stance quad squatters, pecs for chest benchers, back for back-dominant pullers). [313]
- Advanced: longer peaking phases, compete more often; now bring up limiting weak points (e.g. hamstrings for a quad squatter). [313–315]

### 4. Exercise selection by status [315, 319–320]
- Beginner: wide range of variants, all patterns/muscles.
- Intermediate: "Mostly variants targeting the improvement of highly responsive and contributory movements and muscles (genetic proclivities) with 'back burner' work in other areas." E.g. responsive quads → lots of leg press, front squat, high bar squat.
- Advanced: mostly weak-point variants (e.g. big pecs but stalling at lockout → skull crushers, close grip, slingshot).
- Within a category choose variants that feel/are better for you (DB skull crushers if barbell ones hurt elbows; leg press vs hack squat by knee/hip comfort and where you get sore) — both build the general quad mass a strength phase needs. [319–320]

### 5. Technique / lifting style
- Fundamentals fixed (brace, lordotic spine, retracted scapulae, no elbow bend in DL, no knee cave); details individual: short torso → wider squat stance; long arms → less hip drop in DL, lower touch point and more arch-back bar path in bench. [316, 330]
- Choose comp-lift style (stance, grip, pull style) by structure and proclivities once intermediate; e.g. strong triceps → closer bench grip, dominant pecs → wider grip. [320]
- Caveats: don't just keep the style you were taught as a beginner — experiment up to intermediate level; and "Don't just do what 'feels right'": "almost everyone can low bar more than they can high bar" — pick the style that reliably gives bigger numbers. [321–322]

### Finding your own MRV and progression rate [322–323]
- "Keep track of total volumes at any given intensity range, start with the basic recommendation in this book, and track your progress."
- "If you're recovering easily and hitting expected numbers week after week, slowly raise the set numbers until you're regularly overreaching in the week before deloads. That is your MRV for that phase/intensity range" — reuse similar set numbers next time.
- Progression rate is individual: some do well with +15 lb/week, others +5 lb/week. Slower progression → higher starting volume/intensity but lower peak; faster progression → lower start, higher pre-deload peak. Some get beat up by a high starting volume of new movements (start lower, bigger jumps); others handle a high start but overreach on big jumps.

### Importance rank
- Ranked last: humans "respond to training in incredibly homogenous ways"; differences are "to what degree they respond to training and detraining, not if they do or how they do." [316–317]

### Under-application
- Same workout as a training partner: same exercises violates directed variation; same set numbers violates overload or fatigue management — "likely responsible for a large percentage of the cases in which someone says a program 'didn't work for them'"; same reps only OK in the same block and faster-twitch lifters favour the low end of a rep range (sets of 8 vs 12 in hypertrophy); same weight is "the most absurd." [323–325]
- Training like the elite: they have extreme leverages; are more fast-twitch so benefit from lower reps/volumes ("that elite powerlifter may grow almost maximal muscle size from sets of 5, but you will likely benefit most from at least 8 reps per set"); spend more time at high loads ("do what they did to get to the top, not what they're doing to stay there"); have monumentally overloading sessions; need heavy sessions less often (Vince Urbank deadlifts heavy every 2–3 weeks — "you can likely train heavy more often"); focus on weak points. [325–328]
- Training like you used to: stimulus needs change with training age; update old programs rather than rerun verbatim. [328–329]
- Internet programs verbatim: prefer flexible ones; adjust rigid ones as long as the principles still hold. [329]

### Over-application
- Technique universals are not negotiable (lumbar lordosis in squat/DL, hips back + knees forward, scapulae retracted, straight elbows in DL, no knee cave). [330–331]
- "Individualising" into violations of specificity (sled pushes instead of squats/leg presses), overload (one-arm rope extensions vs barbell overhead extensions), fatigue management: "If you don't need to deload, you're not training hard enough." [331–332]
- "X didn't work for me": usually one feature was off. "Take the same Sheiko plan and reduce the set numbers by 1/3. All of a sudden, results are amazing." Westside for raw lifters works if bands/chains on dynamic day are replaced by full-ROM raw assistance lifts. [332–334]
- Summary: almost all individual differences are handled by modifying "volume, progression rate, exercise selection and phase-focus." [334]

## Chapter 10 — Periodization for Powerlifting (pp. 337–343)

The chapter is four tables, one per block, plus the taper design. All numbers below are reproduced. (RP said it would offer digital templates generating programs from these tables. [337])

### Hypertrophy block [338]
- Main purpose: add muscle mass, improve anaerobic work capacity.
- Position in macro: after active rest, before the strength block.
- Average length: 3 weeks to 6 months; usually around 2–3 months for most intermediates.
- Who needs it: most benefit to beginners, second to intermediates, least to advanced.
- Lift selection: overloading variants of the competition lifts targeting needed muscles. Isolation work allowed up to 30% of the program vs 70% minimum compound work.
- Minimum overload intensities: 60% 1RM minimum, 75% 1RM highest recommended for most; some tolerate up to 80%.
- Average set volume: 15–30 working sets per body part per week; compounds count for multiple muscles (close grip bench counts for chest and triceps).
- Weight and set progressions: +5–20 lb per week (2.5–5% per week); 0–1 set added per week in the accumulation phase.
- Average mesocycle length: recommended start 5 weeks total (4 accumulation + 1 deload); more advanced lifters may only tolerate 3:1.
- Indicators of MRV: when reps at 60–75% start falling below the 8-rep range and you can't match last week's reps.
- Light session guidelines: volume 50% of overload day, intensity 50% of overload day.
- Deload guidelines: first half of microcycle — volume 50% of overload day, intensity 90% of overload day; second half — volume 50%, intensity 50%.
- Average overload frequency: 2–6 overloading sessions per body part per week.

### Basic strength block [339]
- Main purpose: increase general strength.
- Position: after hypertrophy, before peaking.
- Average length: 3 weeks to 6 months; usually around 2–3 months for most intermediates (note: Chapter 8 said 3–4 months for intermediates [290]).
- Who needs it: most benefit to intermediates; equal importance to beginners and advanced.
- Lift selection: overloading variants of the competition lifts targeting needed movements; limited or no isolation; "competition lifts often included in later mesocycles."
- Intensities: 75% 1RM minimum, 90% 1RM highest recommended for most.
- Set volume: 10–20 working sets per body part per week; compounds count for multiple muscles.
- Progressions: +5–20 lb per week (2.5–5%); few or no sets added each week.
- Mesocycle length: recommended start 4 weeks total (3 accumulation + 1 deload); more advanced lifters may only tolerate 2:1 (rare).
- Indicators of MRV: rep performance at 75–90% falls below the 3-rep range and you can't match last week's reps.
- Light session: volume 70% of overload day, intensity 70% of overload day.
- Deload: first half of microcycle — volume 50%, intensity 90% of overload day; second half — volume 50%, intensity 50%.
- Overload frequency: 2–4 overloading sessions per body part per week, "with the possibility of light session interspersed."

### Peaking block [340]
- Main purpose: fine-tune technique and nervous-system force production for 1RM lifting.
- Position: after basic strength, before meet and active rest.
- Average length: 3 weeks to 3 months; usually 1–2 months for intermediates.
- Who needs it: most benefit to advanced, second to intermediates, least to beginners.
- Lift selection: mostly the competition lifts; limited or no assistance; at most 70/30 competition lifts to assistance.
- Intensities: 75% 1RM average, split between 85–95% overloading sessions and 50% light sessions.
- Set volume: 5–10 overloading sets per body part per week, plus another 5–10 light-session sets per week possible.
- Progressions: +5–20 lb per week (2.5–5%); few or no sets added during accumulation; taper removes sets.
- Mesocycle length: accumulation can last 3 weeks with one deload week if NOT the taper meso. At most one non-taper peaking meso before the taper, and only for high-level intermediates/advanced; "beginners should ONLY do a taper meso right after a strength phase."
- Indicators of MRV: rep performance and technique stability at 85–95% "start falling off drastically."
- Light session: volume 90% of overload day, intensity 50% of overload day.
- Deload (non-taper meso): first half of microcycle — volume 90%, intensity 50% of overload day; second half — volume 50%, intensity 50%. Taper meso: see taper design.
- Overload frequency: 1–3 overloading sessions per body part per week, with light sessions likely interspersed.

### Taper design [341–342]
- Identical to Chapter 8: Part 1 (normal or 1.5–2× overreach volume), Part 2 (volume 90→50%, intensity maintained/raised), Part 3 (50% volume, 50% intensity); category points and durations as listed above (Cat 1 = 1 wk, Cat 2 = 2 wk, Cat 3 = 3 wk).

### Active rest block [343]
- Main purpose: let fatigue dissipate, especially connective tissue damage and chemical-messenger disruption, while conserving fitness.
- Position: after the meet, before the hypertrophy phase.
- Length: 1–3 weeks; 1 week better for beginners, up to 3 for advanced. "Just one mesocycle is appropriate in almost all cases (1-3 weeks total)."
- Who needs it: most benefit to advanced, second to intermediates, least to beginners.
- Lift selection: mostly the NON-competition lifts; lots of flexibility/mobility work and technique work on new movements.
- Intensity: 50% 1RM average, most sessions around 50% and not much more.
- Volume: 5–10 total sets per body part per week.
- Progressions: none. Light session / deload: N/A (every session light, every microcycle a deload).
- Indicator volume is too high: "rapid drops in fatigue are not noted."
- Frequency: 2–4 sessions per body part per week, all light, no overloads.

### What the Chapter 10 tables imply about a week (only explicit within-week rule in these chapters)
- Deload microcycles are split: first half keeps intensity high (90% of overload-day load in hypertrophy/strength) at half the volume; second half halves both volume and intensity. [338–340]
- Light sessions are defined relative to the overload day: 50%/50% (hypertrophy), 70%/70% (strength), 90% volume / 50% intensity (peaking). [338–340]

## Chapter 11 — Myths, Fallacies & Fads (pp. 344–371)
One line each: myth → book's position.

1. **Bands & chains for raw lifting** → built by/for geared lifters; raw lifts are missed at the bottom, so overloading the top end is "rarely necessary"; specific raw specialty work should use longer pauses and worse leverages; max useful supramaximal overload is 10% over 1RM, used sparingly; bands change bar path; the most advanced raw lifters use "a relatively small and focused pool of exercises" — "specificity is king." [345–347]
2. **Super-high-frequency ("Bulgarian") for everyone** → designed for weightlifting, used mainly in peaking, by small (<100 kg), drugged lifters; USSR got equal results without it. Useful as a 2–3 week concentrated-loading/overreach block for small fast-recovering lifters, PED users, and beginners; if trying it: watch fatigue and back off early, start with 2–3 weeks then return to normal for several weeks, max ~1 month later, never >2 months; daily "max" with no psyching up; it is meant to overreach you just over MRV, not to be sustainable. [347–350]
3. **Cardio makes you great / kills gains** → neither. Needed only enough to do 10s in the hypertrophy phase without cramping; active-rest activity + not being over-fat (20%+) is sufficient; strength/peaking phases need none extra; if needed, use swimming, biking, incline walking, not running; keep it under ~1,500 kcal/week (more "is likely to be of a net negative tradeoff"); best placed in the active-rest block; judge by lift improvement, not by "feeling better." Over-fat lifters should get leaner rather than fitter. [350–353]
4. **"Just shut up and lift" over-simplification** → plan from principles, then execute hard while thinking critically. [353–354]
5. **Hyper-concern with details, missing the big picture** → spend most effort on specificity, overload and fatigue management; phase potentiation and individual differences get far less. Examples of misplaced focus: toe angle while squatting half-depth; 2.5 vs 5 lb on dumbbell moves while not adding sets; leg extension vs adductor order while avoiding front squats; reading elite fatigue literature while training at 2/3 of MRV and skipping sessions. "before you concern yourself with the details of progressions, ask if an overload is being applied at all." [354–355]
6. **Mobility work to the extreme** → you need only enough flexibility to do the lifts with proper technique (below parallel without rounding ≈ enough for squat); extra costs stability, time, may interfere with adaptation, and pre-session mobilising "can decrease force output" — don't do more than you do now, "especially not before hard training." [355–357]
7. **Powerlifting + CrossFit + everything** → beginners can; intermediates will see slower gains (other activity eats the MRV available for powerlifting; endurance signalling reverses strength adaptation); for advanced lifters "even small amounts of extraneous physical activity will interfere." [358–360]
8. **The falling female weight class** → dropping classes for appearance is fine if admitted; doing it believing it improves performance is usually false and carries psychological/health risk. [360–363]
9. **The male bloat project** → vanity muscles don't help and cost time/energy; size changes leverages (helps bench, can hurt deadlift, gut can round the back); bloats hurt raw lifters (fainting, proprioception); realistic gain: 10 lb muscle/yr is "very impressive" and only for relative beginners; gain 1–2 lb/week in gaining phases and diet fat off periodically; large bodyweight shortens life and causes apnea. [364–366]
10. **Gym toys** → Bosu balls and wobble bars reduce force production and don't transfer to heavy-load stability; kettlebells are fine as loaded resistance but underload large muscles (quads) — "it's just weight with a handle." [366–368]
11. **Random day-to-day training** → autoregulated light sessions when needed are fine, and re-prioritising for weeks at a time is fine, but "If you train at random, you get random results. The more you can intelligently plan your training for at least a mesocycle at a time, the better." [368]
12. **Magic programs / "programs are pointless"** → a program is only as good as its principles; "every program works if you train hard" is true only for beginners, wrong "at least half of the time" for intermediates; most programs become effective once a few variables are adjusted for the individual; names (GVT, DUP) are not the mechanism — DUP works via "intra-microcycle volume variation" that aids recovery/adaptation. [369–371]

## Autoregulation statements in these chapters (collected)
- Light sessions "when needed ala autoregulation" are explicitly endorsed, but the plan should still be written for at least a mesocycle. [368]
- MRV indicators that tell you a planned session/meso is too much: hypertrophy — reps at 60–75% fall below 8 and you can't match last week; strength — reps at 75–90% fall below 3 and you can't match last week; peaking — rep performance and technique stability at 85–95% fall off drastically; active rest — fatigue isn't dropping rapidly. [338–340, 343]
- MRV-finding loop: if recovering easily and hitting expected numbers week after week, add sets slowly until you regularly overreach in the pre-deload week; that is your MRV; reuse those set numbers next time. [322]
- High-frequency blocks: "If you feel like you're overreaching early in the program, back off." [350]
- "If you don't need to deload, you're not training hard enough." [332]
- Cardio dose: watch lift improvement rates, not just how you feel. [353]
- Exercise choice by response: keep the variant that gets you sore in the target muscle with joints feeling fine; drop the one that gives joint pain "and not much else." [320]
- Technique stale/"feels off" and not improving week to week → drop that comp lift for several weeks in favour of variants. [248]

## Cardio / conditioning, session length, warm-ups, rest, assistance (collected)
- Cardio: see Myth 3 — ≤1,500 kcal/week, low-impact modalities, placed in active rest; only enough to tolerate sets of 10 in hypertrophy. [351–353]
- Session length: only mentioned negatively — a session trying to train peaking + strength + hypertrophy for one lift would run "3 hours" and be unsustainable. [303]
- Warm-ups: no prescription; only that pre-session mobility/stretching can lower force output. [357]
- Rest times: no prescription; strength/peaking described as "lower rep, longer rest time" phases and hypertrophy as short-rest/metabolite work. [250, 351]
- Assistance selection/volume: isolation ≤30% of program in hypertrophy (≥70% compound); limited/none in strength; assistance ≤30% in peaking; prefer heavily loadable moves (front squat, leg press, close grip, DB flye, skull crusher, rows, pull-ups, block pulls, SLDL) over low-overload ones (leg extension, band laterals, reverse curls, one-arm cable work); compounds count toward multiple muscles' weekly set totals. [271–272, 338–340]
- Mobility: only as much as needed for technique; mostly in active rest. [343, 357]

## Numbers most relevant to a 4-day, fatigue-averse program (no meet)

1. **Sequence without a meet:** alternate hypertrophy ↔ strength blocks for "several 6 week to one-year macrocycles"; peaking is optional and "only a good idea if you've got something to show for it." [285]
2. **Block lengths for an intermediate:** hypertrophy ~2–3 months, strength ~2–4 months; never under 3 weeks per phase; insert a lower-volume meso by ~3 months of hypertrophy; cap exclusive strength at ~3 (max 6) months. [284–285, 289–290, 300–301, 338–339]
3. **Mesocycle shape:** hypertrophy 4 weeks accumulation + 1 deload (5 total); strength 3 + 1 (4 total); harder-recovering lifters drop to 3:1 / 2:1. [338–339]
4. **Weekly sets per body part:** hypertrophy 15–30; strength 10–20; peaking 5–10 (+5–10 light); active rest 5–10 total. Compounds count for every muscle they load. [338–340, 343]
5. **Intensity and reps:** hypertrophy 60–75% (some to 80%), 6–10 reps, avoid "far over 10"; strength 75–90%, 3–6 reps; peaking 85–95% overload / 50% light, 1–3 reps. [289–290, 338–340]
6. **Overload sessions per muscle per week:** hypertrophy 2–6; strength 2–4 (light sessions may be interspersed); peaking 1–3. [338–340]
7. **Weekly progression:** +5–20 lb (2.5–5%) per week on the overload sets; add 0–1 set/week in hypertrophy, few/none in strength; pick the slower progression (e.g. +5 lb) with a higher start if big jumps overreach you. [322, 338–339]
8. **Stop/adjust signals (MRV reached):** can't match last week's reps and reps drop below 8 at 60–75% (hypertrophy) or below 3 at 75–90% (strength). Fatigue-averse rule: aim to reach overreach only in the last accumulation week, then deload. [322, 338–339]
9. **Deload week recipe:** first half of the week — 50% of overload-day volume at 90% of overload-day intensity; second half — 50% volume at 50% intensity. (Peaking: 90% vol / 50% int, then 50/50.) [338–340]
10. **Light session recipe:** hypertrophy 50% vol / 50% int; strength 70% vol / 70% int; peaking 90% vol / 50% int relative to the overload day. [338–340]
11. **Exercise rotation:** change variants once per mesocycle, not weekly; keep a variant for a full meso; drop the competition lift entirely for a meso or more during hypertrophy/early strength and bring it back in later strength mesos. [250, 253, 256, 269, 339]
12. **What counts as a different variant:** front vs high-bar vs low-bar only; grips/stances ≥3–4 in (one hand/foot width) apart. [265]
13. **Phase purity:** ≥70% of work phase-specific, ≤30% other (e.g. a little strength work at the end of a hypertrophy block, a little extra volume in a strength block). Isolation ≤30% in hypertrophy, little/none in strength. [286, 338–339]
14. **Exercise selection for an intermediate:** mostly variants that build your responsive/contributing muscles, with "back burner" work elsewhere; pick heavily loadable moves and the ones your joints tolerate. [271–272, 313, 319–320]
15. **Within-week rep variation is fine if it stays in one range** (e.g. 6/4/2 in a strength block); don't mix 12s, 6s and 2s in one week. [274–276]
16. **Outside-the-gym load:** cardio ≤1,500 kcal/week, low impact, best in active rest; other sports slow intermediate progress; no extra mobility work, none before lifting. [351–353, 357–358]
17. **Active rest between macrocycles:** 1–3 weeks (intermediate ≈ 2), ~50% 1RM, 5–10 sets/body part/week, 2–4 light sessions per body part, non-competition lifts. [343]
