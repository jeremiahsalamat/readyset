# CHAPTER 5 — FATIGUE MANAGEMENT (pp. 104–185)

## 5.1 The four sources of cumulative fatigue [104–113]

Training disrupts four classes of physiology: 1) fuel stores, 2) nervous system, 3) chemical messengers, 4) tissue structure [104]. With any effective training frequency not all fatigue dissipates between sessions, so it accumulates ("cumulative fatigue") [104–105].

**Fuel stores (glycogen)** [105–107]
- Reps of 10 and below are fueled by phosphagens + glycolysis; phosphagens recover within minutes and are not a cumulative-fatigue player [105].
- Low glycogen causes: diminished intensity ("especially in reps over 3"), diminished volume (can't complete multiple heavy sets), increased perceived effort, anabolic signaling turned down [106].
- "The biggest factor in glycogen depletion is the total volume of training, and the intensity is a much smaller factor." Total work = sets × reps × weight [106–107].
- DOMS directly interferes with glucose uptake and glycogen synthesis [107].
- No single session depletes glycogen enough for a detectable performance drop [107].
- "Impactful reductions of glycogen usually take weeks of powerlifting training to accumulate, but they only take days to resolve." [107]
- Repletion: more dietary carbs, reduced training volume, reduced DOMS; a few days at most [107].

**Nervous system** [108–109]
- Fatigued NS effects: lower neural drive/force output; poorly coordinated firing → bar instability and technique breakdown at heavier loads; reduced learning efficiency [109].
- Volume is the primary determinant; intensity has an incrementally higher effect at constant volume: "10 sets of 1 rep at 90%" is significantly more NS-fatiguing than "one set of 14 reps at 65%" at near-identical volume [109].
- To reduce NS fatigue volume "must absolutely come down", intensity decreases likely also help; recovery takes "between days and weeks of less voluminous and lighter training" [109].

**Chemical messengers** [109–113]
- AMPk is activated by higher-volume (not high-intensity) training "and all forms of cardio", hypocaloric/low-glycogen states; high AMPk directly blunts mTOR [111].
- With prolonged high-volume training testosterone gradually declines and cortisol rises over weeks to months [112–113].
- "While intensity does not greatly affect chemical messengers associated with fatigue, volume does, and in a big way." It is "consistently high volume with little deviation" that sums them; they take weeks to rise and weeks of lower-volume training to fall [113].
- Powerlifters mostly in 3–5 rep strength work at moderate volumes are not the most affected; those adding lots of bodybuilding volume or running hypertrophy phases are [113].

**Tissue structures** [113]: muscle cell structures/proteins, fascia, tendons, ligaments, bones.

**Tool-to-timescale mapping** [115]
- Light sessions and non-training days → glycogen restoration.
- Deload weeks → nervous system fatigue, some cellular messengers, muscle tissue damage.
- Low-volume phases → long-timescale messengers (testosterone, cortisol).
- Active rest phases → connective tissue (fascia, tendon, ligament, bone) and some psychological fatigue.

## 5.2 The three fatigue states [116–119]

- At any time a lifter is in exactly one of: (A) adequate recovery (training at or below MRV), (B) overreaching, (C) overtraining [116].
- (A) At or below MRV: fatigue not interfering with adaptation or performance, injury risk not elevated; "almost no need to fatigue manage in this state" [116].
- (B) **Non-functional overreaching**: passing MRV because recovery dropped (e.g., "you're not getting enough sleep this week"), with no adaptation benefit [116–117].
- (B) **Functional overreaching**: intentionally pushing "just over your body's ability to recover, and then pull it back"; short-term; caused by over-working, not under-recovering [117].
- Supercompensation can occur over several weeks "if a couple of those weeks are just beyond MRV and the next one or two are way below it" [117].
- Normal mesocycle pattern: "the last week of 4 weeks of hard training may be, as a week, technically over MRV (overreaching). The deload week that follows reduces fatigue" [117].
- "If overreaching occurs for too long (in most powerlifting training, over 2-3 weeks), overtraining may result." [118]
- (C) **Net-neutral overtraining**: catch it "within the first month"; may take up to two months to recover; requires "weeks (perhaps a whole month)" of very light, low-volume training "somewhere in the vicinity of half the MRV", then several more weeks to re-establish fitness [118].
- (C) **Net-negative overtraining**: several months to a year to recover; lasting injuries; "profoundly rare in powerlifting" because lifters get hurt before they overtrain that far [119].

## 5.3 Importance rank [121]
- Fatigue management is the 3rd-ranked principle (after Specificity and Overload); with those three alone "you're going to have a long and productive career" [121].

## 5.4 MRV: definition, phase-specific signs, mesocycle framing [123–127]

- MRV of a time period: the maximum tolerable volume such that "any lower volumes are not maximally overloading and any higher volumes lead to a decrease in performance" [123].
- Fatigue "rises faster and faster with every microcycle" when overload is progressive; performance is the first thing it interferes with [123].
- Four phase-specific MRVs [123, 125–126]:
  - **Hypertrophy MRV**: volume above which muscle size decreases [123]. Highest of the four [125]. Signs you have surpassed it [125]:
    - can't maintain usual reps with 60–75% 1RM weights
    - no longer get good pumps
    - "dull, achy, and tired the next day after training instead of sore"
    - feel depleted/unenergetic in workouts, struggling to meet minimum work
  - **Strength MRV**: volume above which basic strength decreases; lower than hypertrophy MRV [123, 126]. Test: "When you can't match your recent performances in sets of 3-6 reps ... you're at or just past your MRV." Example: 405×5,5,5 (wk 1) → 415×5,5,5 (wk 2) → only 425×3,2,2 → MRV passed [126].
  - **Peaking MRV**: far lower than hypertrophy and strength; if heavy sets of 3 are hit as expected with good technique you're at/below; if raising volume produces missed reps and technical breakdown you've surpassed it [126].
  - **Active recovery MRV**: the most volume you can do and still recover at the best rate; "may be as little as 1/4 the volume of the hypertrophy phase" [126].
- MRV is a **per-mesocycle** concept; single-session MRV is "rather meaningless" [127].
- Mesocycle shape: weeks start below MRV and come "closer and closer", MRV is reached in the middle, intentionally surpassed "towards the end of most mesocycles", then a deload far below it [127]. The average MRV "is usually about the same volume as the middle-end microcycles" [127].
- Rationale for ramping rather than starting at MRV: "preventing us from deloading every other week by always trying to train for MRV" [127].

## 5.5 Factors that change MRV and fatigue dynamics [130–143]

**Recovery-side and lifestyle** [130–131]
- Sleep and nutrition raise MRV from both sides (train harder and recover faster) [130].
- "Life stress and other physical activities may not impede hard training as much, but do come with a high price for recovery" [130].
- A high-volume work-capacity/hypertrophy phase before a strength phase raises strength-phase work capacity but does little for recovery [130].
- Training for months/years raises MRV via work capacity; don't copy elite routines verbatim [130].
- Individual variance: "Some lifters can get three hours of sleep and smash workouts, while still others miss one hour out of eight and start to overreach." Doing the same sets as your partner is theoretically flawed [131].

**Proximity to failure** [132–133]
- Failure proximity has a very high effect on fatigue; at equal volume and intensity, sets to failure are more fatiguing; e.g., a 5RM ground out in one set sums more fatigue than the same 5 reps as singles with 3 min rest [132].
- "failure training lowers the MRV" [132]; HIT's low volume follows from this and is "inferior for both outcomes" [132–133].
- Best training is "close to failure ... but not at failure" [133].
- Exception: failure (or very close) "may be tailor-made for the final microcycle of accumulation training" because a deload follows [133].

**Lift type** [134–136]
- Machines and isolation moves disrupt less than free-weight compounds; if fatigue must be kept down, machines are an option [134].
- Fatigue ranking: Deadlift most (heaviest weights, by far most muscle), Squat intermediate (longest ROM), Bench least (shortest ROM, lightest, least muscle) [135].
- Implications: higher frequency helps benching more than deadlifting, squat in between; when peaking, lower training load for deadlift first, squat second, bench last [136].

**Career proximity to peak** [136–137]: more advanced → fatigue sums quicker and falls slower; lower frequencies for experienced lifters "may be somewhat warranted" [137].

**Body size** [137–138]
- Bigger lifters accumulate fatigue faster/dissipate slower; "Bigger lifters usually need lower frequencies and longer tapers" [138].
- Taller lifters do more work per rep. Example: Kiril Sarychev (6'8", 396 lb, sets of 5 at 600+) benches heavy "only about every week and a half" [138].

**Strength** [139]
- Stronger lifters sum fatigue faster and dissipate slower. Westside programmed one heavy upper and one heavy lower session per week [139].
- "Squat everyday" works best in short "concentrated loading" periods of several weeks and for not-the-strongest lifters; the strongest lifters use the lowest frequencies [139].
- As strength rises: either train less frequently or increase volume-load undulation within the microcycle [139].

**Fiber type** [139–140]: fast-twitch muscles heal slower; hamstrings are notoriously fast-twitch (part of why deadlift is so fatiguing); upper body is faster-twitch than quads/glutes [140].

**Gender** [141–142]: females may recover slightly faster; dominant difference is smaller size, shorter ROM, lower absolute strength [142].

**Technique** [142–143]: poor leverage/technique increases fatigue; "letting your technique slip just to grind out a few more reps may be a very costly move to your fatigue state"; good technique can raise MRV [142–143].

## 5.6 Training-based fatigue-management tools [143–156]

Four tools stratified by timescale: rest days, light sessions, deloads, active rest [143].

### Rest/off days [143–147]
- Research: a light, low-volume session "may actually attenuate physiological fatigue more effectively than a day off"; the difference is small [143–144].
- Rest days' main advantage is **psychological** fatigue reduction (packing, commuting, warming up all carry stress) [144].
- How many: "One day per week is the likely minimum"; 7-day programs almost absent from top approaches; the authors find "two days off per week seems to be noticeably more effective", especially for drug-free lifters and when the two days are **back-to-back** [145].
- Overload sessions should be spread evenly across the microcycle: squatting heavy Monday, benching heavy Tuesday, deadlifting heavy Wednesday is worse than Mon/Wed/Fri [146].
- Conclusion: "At least one rest day should be taken, possibly two" with the overload pattern otherwise spread fairly evenly [146].
- Rest days restore glycogen; chemical messengers are largely unaltered; little connective-tissue healing [146–147].

### Light sessions [147–151]
- Definition: a session "that does not present an overload and thus does not sum any additional fatigue"; far enough below MRV to dissipate fatigue, high enough to prevent fitness loss [147].
- Benefits: promote recovery/adaptation, skill practice without fatigue, prevent fitness decay between sessions [147].
- "Middle of the road" sessions neither reduce fatigue nor adapt — the worst option [148].
- Volume has the biggest effect on fatigue; intensity has the biggest effect on conserving adaptations; so light sessions "must in most circumstances in actuality be low-volume sessions that are still heavy enough to conserve adaptations" [148].
- Prescription (as % of the overload day) [148–149]:

| Phase | Volume | Intensity |
|---|---|---|
| Hypertrophy | 50% | 90% |
| Strength | 70% | 70% |
| Peaking | 90% | 50% |

- Mistake 1 (most common): reduce intensity but raise volume — e.g., normal 5×5 @100 lb vs "light" 5×10 @60 lb is more volume; fatigue ends up about the same, with no stimulus [149].
- Mistake 2: not dropping intensity enough when weights are "well over 80% 1RM" — 3×3 instead of 3×5 is not enough of a reduction [150].
- Usage: optional; "Light sessions can be used 1-2 times per week"; more use in strength and peaking phases; "In hypertrophy training, the use of light sessions has no strong theoretical backing" [150].
- Placement: "one or two light sessions can be placed at the end of a microcycle" in strength/peaking phases [151].
- Effects mainly on glycogen and nervous system, minimal on messengers/tissue [151].

### Deloads [151–154]
- Needed because NS fatigue, messenger disruption and muscle/fascia microtears are not reduced within several days [151].
- Length: "the average deload will take an entire microcycle ... about one week" [152].
- Must have a lower average intensity than light sessions; make the **latter half very low intensity** so healing tissue is not re-torn [152].
- Prescription (as % of the overload day) [152–153]:

| Phase | First half of week | Second half of week |
|---|---|---|
| Hypertrophy | Vol 50% / Int 90% | Vol 50% / Int 50% |
| Strength | Vol 70% / Int 70% | Vol 50% / Int 50% |
| Peaking | Vol 90% / Int 50% | Vol 50% / Int 50% |

- Exact percentages not required; essentials: "some meaningful reductions in both volume and intensity" [153].
- Final peaking mesocycle before a meet: use a taper instead [153].
- When: at the end of each mesocycle, "which can last between 3 and 5 weeks of accumulation in most cases" [153–154].
- After a proper deload: glycogen restocked (iso/hyper-caloric diet), NS "almost completely healed", cortisol/testosterone back to sustainable levels, muscle/fascia microtears healed [154].

### Active rest [154–156, 180–182]
- Over a macrocycle the CNS may recover only 90–95% with each deload; connective-tissue microtears accumulate [154].
- Length: "should usually average around two weeks"; later described as "A period of 2-4 weeks" [155, 181].
- Prescription: Volume 50% / Intensity 50% of overload day throughout (no moderate-intensity first half) [155].
- Timing: right after a big meet, once or twice a year (bigger/stronger/more experienced → twice) [155]. Taking the first week fully off and the second as active rest is fine, but two full weeks of active rest is slightly better [155].
- Goal-directed content: technique work, flexibility, rehab [181–182].

## 5.7 Autoregulation vs proactive fatigue management [156–161]

- Proactive tools assume accurate prediction of fatigue; real life (moving furniture, stressful eating, poor sleep, a tender pec) breaks that [156–157].
- Monitoring methods: "performance indicators" (most straightforward), "morning resting heart rate, desire to train, hunger, and sex drive" [157].
- Example: last accumulation week planned 5×5 @200 kg finishes "with an easy 2-3 reps left in the tank on each set" → is it really time to deload? [157]
- Two constraints: the **accumulation:deload ratio** (extra off days/light sessions/deloads shrink productive time) [158] and **training-plan interference** (extending a hypertrophy phase by 2 weeks steals from an 8-week strength phase) [158–159].
- Recommendations [159–160]:
  - a) Know your MRV: "Don't train by feel and you won't have to fatigue manage by feel." If you think you can recover from 10×10 squats but 6×10 is your real best, you wreck the A:D ratio [159].
  - b) **Too little fatigue**: adjust sets and weights marginally, not weeks. "If you plan an accumulation phase of 4 weeks and week 2 is a breeze, go up 15lbs instead of 10lbs on some week 3 movements, or add a set or two" [159–160].
  - c) **Too much fatigue**: "Use Extra Light Sessions, not Deloads". If week 2 starts much harder than it should, "take the rest of that week as light sessions" [160].
  - "If you need unplanned deloads too often, chances are you're misestimating your mesocycle-scale MRV." [160]
- Experience gating: beginners should use rigid programming; advanced lifters use autoregulation "as a modifier of their programming rather than the dominant feature" [161].

## 5.8 Under-application of fatigue management [161–174]

**Chronic volume excess** [161–166]
- Volume categories (table lost; text gives): a) not enough to adapt; b) enough for some adaptation but not the most; c) at MRV — "the most volume that lifter can benefit from"; d) beyond MRV (functional overreaching, beneficial only short term "lasting perhaps the last week of a mesocycle until a deload") [161, 163]. Exceeding d too often → stalling, regression, potential overtraining [163].
- Three paths: "hardcore" mentality, copying the greats, keeping up with the Joneses [163–166].
- "start with a much lower volume than they use, and ease in. Increasing volume when you're recovered is MUCH easier and safer than decreasing volume when you're already beat up." [164]

**Chronically going too heavy** [167–171]
- Definition of too heavy too often: "more than 8 weeks of training at your beyond 90%1RM leading up to a meet, and any training beyond 90% if the meet is far away" [167].
- Strength training needs heavy (75% 1RM+) work consistently, presented repeatedly in sufficient volume [167].
- Reps available per session: at 95% "5, maybe 8"; at 80% "40? Maybe 50?" [168].
- Example for a 500-lb squatter reaching 4000 lb volume: 4×10 @100 (20%), 4×5 @200 (40%), 5×2 @400 (80%), 9×1 @450 (90%). The 5×2 @80% is repeatable 2–3×/week; the 9 singles @90% "will crush you and leave you beat up for at least a week" [168–169].
- 90% session may make you "maybe about 20% stronger" than the 80% one, but the 80% workout can be repeated "at least twice as often" [169].
- Westside's average intensity works out to about 75% because half of weekly work is ~60% speed work [169].
- "Save the max training for the peaking phase" [171].

**Not taking deloads / active rest** [171–173]
- If you never need to deload, "9 times out of 10" the training is actually too easy; "2 heavy singles a week does not constitute proper, stimulating powerlifting training" [172].
- Without deloads you will overreach "after more than about 8 weeks of proper overload", then lose "about 3 weeks out of every 8" to burnout/injury [172].
- "Do you have to take light sessions? No. ... But deloads? Mandatory, and active rest for at least a week after each meet is a very good idea." [173]

**Deloads that are too overloading** [173–174]
- "The single biggest contributor to fatigue is volume"; deloading by dropping volume only (intensity unchanged) is a common short-term approach in effective programs [173].
- Dropping intensity but raising reps/sets ("get a pump") is a poor deload: adaptations not conserved and "cumulative fatigue at the end of the deload may be higher than before it" [173–174].
- "If your deload is tough, you're not dropping the fatigue you should be" [174].

## 5.9 Over-application of fatigue management [174–182]

**Never pushing hard enough** [174–177]
- Chronic low volume: you always perform well and feel great, "but you never get that much better" [175].
- Not enough intensity: 60% 1RM can promote hypertrophy; 50% is hard to justify even for hypertrophy [175–176]. Assistance work: "If intensity is not above 60%1RM for that exercise, it's likely not going to do much" [176]. Either train it hard enough or drop it for recovery [176].
- Too far from failure: stopping "1 rep short of failure every training session" makes fatigue rise too fast; "8 reps short of failure" means never training hard enough [176]. Rep capacities: ~5 reps at 85% 1RM, ~10 reps at 75%; stopping 8 short at 75% = sets of 2; at 85%+ anything over 4 from failure = singles [177]. Conclusion: stay closer to failure while avoiding failure itself [177].

**Too frequent deloading** [177–178]
- Training far above MRV in weeks 1–2 forces a deload in week 3; repeating this = 2:1 accumulation:deload, "literally slicing off 1/3 of the training year's potential gains"; with tapers and active rest "you're practically training hard only about half of the year" [177–178].
- Better: "go hard enough to get within the maximal overload threshold in week one and make slow progressions week to week", which "often results in a 3:1 or 4:1 accumulation-to-deload paradigm" [178].

**Fatigue management that is too easy** [178–180]
- Too-easy or fully-off periods lose more fitness AND drop fatigue less; "Light training works better than 'barely anything' training or total off time" [179].
- Unplanned off days: on a very-high-fatigue day a light session beats a day off for both fatigue and fitness; "if you're too beat up to even do a light session... you need a doctor" [179].
- Off-week replacing a deload is worse than a deload; if psychologically fried, "take the first half of the deload week off and then train light the last half" [180]. Being too fried to show up means volumes/intensities need re-examining [180].
- Undirected active rest: should be 2–4 weeks with volume and intensity reduced by a wide margin, used for technique, flexibility, rehab [181–182].

## 5.10 Chapter 5 summary / key points [183–184]
- Fatigue is unavoidable with overload; reduce it at regular intervals with rest days, light sessions, deloads, active rest [183].
- Best start is "a reasonable estimate of your MRV for the different intensities of training (size vs. strength vs. peaking)"; MRV is not static and "needs constant monitoring" [183].
- Fatigue management is individual: "What works for some may not work for everyone" [184].

# CHAPTER 6 — STIMULUS-RECOVERY-ADAPTATION (pp. 186–242)

## 6.1 Definition [186–189]
- SRA = stimulus, recovery, adaptation; sport-science derivation of Selye's GAS [186].
- Stimuli that disrupt the most and depress performance longest produce the most adaptation, but the whole cycle takes longer [188].
- During recovery, further overloads depress performance further; if within long-term ability this is overreaching, if too great it yields incomplete recovery with little/no adaptation [188].
- "overload training should be followed by periods of rest or non-overloading training in order to prevent interference with recovery" [188].
- Adaptation usually concludes after recovery; "It is almost always easier to regain older performances than it is to establish new ones" [189].
- "Session-rest-session" paradigm: schedule the next session "at the adaptive peak of the generated SRA curve of the previous session" [189].

## 6.2 SRA curves per system [191–198]
- **Technique (nervous system)**: adaptations "rarely need longer than a day to complete, and often much less"; technique alone could be trained multiple times per day; holding technique needs only several sessions a week [191].
- **Hypertrophy**: curve "usually measured in days"; for size alone "we would overload somewhere between 2 and 4 times per week" [193]. Pennation-angle changes take weeks and run a similar course [193].
- **Nervous-system force production**: "takes about a week to peak in adaptation", with bigger/stronger/more experienced lifters "to the tune of two weeks at the higher volumes and intensities"; for force alone, overload once per week or more rarely (Westside: once per week per muscle group) [195].
- Session type and which curve it elevates most [198]: light technique practice → technical; hypertrophy training → muscle growth; strength training → NS force; peaking (low volume, 90%+) → technical and force considerably, connective tissue the most.

## 6.3 Powerlifting definition [205]
- Give enough rest/non-overloading time to the system being trained; waiting too long lets the curve fall back to baseline [205].
- Harder session = longer curve: "a very tough hypertrophy session can take 3 days for the whole SRA cycle to complete, while an easier one might take only 2 days" [205].
- "multiple frequencies are ok, so long as you give more rest time between sessions if your sessions are harder (usually higher in volume ...)" [205].

## 6.4 Importance rank [207]
- SRA is 4th; first principle "not essential to successful training". Any non-insane frequency ("between once a day and once a week") trains all four systems and gives good, if not best, results [207].

## 6.5 Recommended overloading frequencies by goal [209]
- Technique: several sessions/day down to ~4 overloading sessions/week.
- Hypertrophy: 4 to 2 overloading sessions per week.
- Basic strength: 3 to 1 overloading sessions per week, "with at least 1 light session per week if down to 1 overloading session to conserve hypertrophy and technique".
- Peaking: 2 to 1 overloading sessions per week, with at least 1 light session.
- Connective tissue: "A break from training past 80% 1RM for at least a month every 4 months or so".
- Also: muscle-size accretion lasts one to several days post-session, so train a muscle "every one to several days"; multi-a-day or once-per-week training produces worse results than possible [209].

## 6.6 Sources of frequency variance [210–220]

- **Session volume** [210–212]: lower per-session volume → shorter curve → higher possible frequency. Example: 8 sets of squats every 4 days vs 4 sets every 2 days — each gives about half the adaptive peak but twice the workouts in the same span (conclusion page lost). "there is a good bit of leeway on effective training frequencies by simply spreading out or clustering the workload, so long as the total work is still the same" [212].
- **Technical proficiency** [212–214]: beginners benefit from high frequency (technique, early neural gains) [212–213]; for advanced lifters "it becomes difficult to construct an argument for more frequent lifting than about twice a week (for each movement)"; extra days may be better taken off [213].
- **Fiber type** [214]: hamstrings faster-twitch than quads → may benefit from lower frequency; high-frequency squatting works for newer/smaller lifters, high-frequency deadlifting does not [214].
- **Muscle size/architecture** [215–216]: small muscles (biceps, rear delts) can be overloaded nearly daily; quads cannot at decent volume; upper-body pulling tolerated "multiple times per week, often with every training session"; chest and glutes "almost every session is essentially unheard of" [215–216].
- **Strength** [216]: stronger lifters have longer NS and connective-tissue curves and "gravitate naturally towards lower frequencies".
- **Psych-up** [216–217]: lifters who psych up more generate longer curves and need lower frequency; calm lifters should train more often.
- **Lift-specific** [217–220]:
  - Squat: daily squatting sustainable for short periods for smaller/less strong lifters; even bigger/stronger lifters do "quad-heavy work at least twice a week, with only once a week of any kind of quad stimulus being quite rare" [219].
  - Bench: smaller/less strong lifters can overload bench "up to 4x a week"; stronger lifters "rarely overload the bench more than once per week" (Sarychev: every ~1.5 weeks); most train bench muscles at least 2×/week, but the second session is usually not an overload of the bench press itself — assistance lifts are overloaded instead [219].
  - Deadlift / OHP / hamstrings: long curves; deadlift uses "something like 80% of the skeletal muscles"; heavy deadlifts "can only be programmed once a week for most lifters", strongest deadlifters overload "as infrequently as every two weeks" [219–220].
- **Training-mediated** [220]: chronic higher-frequency training makes it more tolerable over time.

## 6.7 Constraints on frequency [220–229]
- **Insufficient overload (high-frequency bound)** [220–223]: each session needs a minimum volume and intensity; 10 tiny sessions may adapt less than one session with 10× the work; a daily max single is an insufficient overload for advanced lifters; "Splitting their weekly training into fewer but more individually overloading sessions may be a better approach" [222–223].
- **Excessive fatigue/overload ratio (low-frequency bound)** [224–225]: once every 2 weeks would need astronomical per-session volume → recovery only, no gain; "stimulate, don't annihilate" [224]. Minimum frequencies: technique "at least two sessions of technique work per week ... (which includes overload sessions themselves)" [224]; hypertrophy — beginners need at least weekly per muscle, which "becomes inadequate within several months"; 1×/week per muscle only makes best gains "if you're over 250lbs of mostly muscle" [225]; neural force output "at least every 1.5 weeks" [225]; connective tissue needs constant sub-maximal stimulation, not infrequent brutal sessions [225].
- **Adaptive dissipation** [228, preceding page lost]: "After the 6th day, some of the muscle from that first session has already been lost. If we train on the 7th day, we're only saving some" [228].
- Result: "If weekly volumes and intensities are being met at close to MRV, the frequency of a program is not a gigantic determinant of success" [228]; neither Westside-style low frequency nor Squat Everyday is categorically better [229].

## 6.8 Arrangement of training days [229–230]
- Same 3×/week lower-body frequency, different outcomes: Mon/Wed/Fri is logical; Mon/Fri/Sat has too much lag Mon→Fri and too little Fri→Sat — workable only if Monday is very hard and Friday very easy (or rely on functional overreaching); Mon/Tue/Wed makes Wednesday unable to overload and leaves a huge gap to the next Monday [229–230].
- Rule: "structuring our workouts in a manner that roughly spaces them out in an even manner is probably the best idea" [230].
- Example: heavy squat Mon, heavy front squat Tue, heavy deadlift + leg press Wed → "your 'heavy' deadlifts are anything but" [230].

## 6.9 Under-application of SRA [231–235]
- **Too high frequency**: high-frequency overload suits technique and hypertrophy goals, i.e., beginners/intermediates, smaller and slower-twitch lifters; superheavyweights and very advanced lifters rarely use it [231]. After technical near-mastery, technique needs only maintenance practice "(1-2x per week)" [232]. A lifter at the top of their weight class gets little from frequent hypertrophy overload; it interferes with strength work [232].
- **Too low frequency**: Westside-style once-a-week-heavy per muscle gave beginners poor technique and little size gain [233–234]. Program frequency to the lifter's state, even if training partners differ [234].
- **Too clustered** [235]. Overloading too soon after the last session: interferes with overload in the present session; leaves a long gap before the next overload (involution more likely); "Directly blunts current adaptations in progress". Overloading too late/too close to the next session: fatigue interferes with the next session. Applies across body parts too (central fatigue): "Training heavy squat, bench, and deadlift on 3 consecutive days is thus mostly unheard of" [235].

## 6.10 Over-application of SRA [236–240]
- Don't wait for every curve to finish: "it's ok to train sore sometimes" (technique focus), "ok to not train at your strongest" (hypertrophy focus), "ok to have sore joints for some periods" (strength focus), as long as fatigue is managed periodically [236].
- Functional overreaching is useful, especially before competition; "If you overreach in week 1 of a strength mesocycle, you're going to run into serious problems"; being averse to all overreaching loses fitness during deloads/tapers [238].
- Perfect SRA symmetry conflicts with Fatigue Management (which needs a distinct recovery stretch each microcycle); "Fatigue Management is a more important training principle, it usually wins"; get close to even spacing "while still leaving some bias in overload session arrangement to allow for fatigue to dissipate" [240].
- Summary: "Rest too little and your performance and adaptations from each session suffer. Rest too long and the progress you've made in past sessions deteriorates" [240].

# Cross-cutting: cardio, sleep, stress, and training capacity (both chapters)
- AMPk (catabolic, mTOR-blunting) is activated by higher-volume training "and all forms of cardio", and by hypocaloric/low-glycogen states [111]. No cardio prescription is given in these chapters.
- Insufficient sleep is the book's example of how non-functional overreaching happens [116]; sleep and nutrition raise MRV from both the work-capacity and recovery sides [130].
- "Life stress and other physical activities ... come with a high price for recovery" — reducing them raises MRV on the recovery side [130]; the best lifters "usually do a good job staying low-stress" [130].
- Psychological stress "adds right into the cumulative fatigue effect" even without tissue damage; this is why rest days (ideally two, back-to-back) matter [144–145].
- Off-plan physical work (e.g., moving furniture) raises glycogen use and justifies autoregulating that week [157].
- Carbohydrate restriction while dieting to a weight class limits glycogen repletion [107].

# Numbers most relevant to a 4-day, fatigue-averse program

1. **Mesocycle shape**: 3–5 weeks of accumulation, then a 1-week deload; start the first week below MRV and ramp toward/just over it only in the final week [127, 153–154]. Aim for 3:1 or 4:1 accumulation:deload; a 2:1 pattern means you started too hard [177–178].
2. **Deload prescription (strength phase)**: first half of the week 70% volume / 70% intensity of the overload day, second half 50% / 50%. Hypertrophy: 50%/90% then 50%/50%. Both volume AND intensity must drop; never raise reps/sets to compensate [152–153, 173–174]. If a deload feels tough it isn't working [174].
3. **Light session prescription**: strength phase 70% volume / 70% intensity; hypertrophy 50% / 90%; use 1–2 per week, placed at the end of the microcycle; never "lighter weight, more reps" [148–151].
4. **Autoregulation rule for a hard week**: if fatigue is too high early in a week, convert the remaining sessions to light sessions rather than taking an unplanned deload or day off [160, 179]. Light session > day off for both fatigue reduction and fitness retention [179].
5. **Autoregulation rule for an easy week**: add a set or two, or increase the jump (e.g., +15 lb instead of +10 lb) on some movements the next week; do not add weeks [159–160].
6. **MRV-passed trigger (strength)**: you cannot match recent performance in sets of 3–6 reps (e.g., planned 5,5,5 becomes 3,2,2) [126]. Hypertrophy-MRV signs: can't hold usual reps at 60–75%, no pumps, dull/achy/tired instead of sore, depleted in sessions [125].
7. **Frequency ranges**: basic strength 1–3 overloading sessions per lift per week (plus ≥1 light session if only 1); hypertrophy 2–4 per muscle per week [209]. For advanced lifters more than ~2×/week per movement is hard to justify [213]. Deadlift heavy once a week for most [220]; bench muscles ≥2×/week but the bench itself typically overloaded once, assistance overloaded in the second session [219]; quad work at least twice a week [219].
8. **Rest days**: at least 1/week, 2 is "noticeably more effective", ideally back-to-back; spread overload sessions evenly; never heavy squat/bench/deadlift on three consecutive days [145–146, 235].
9. **Session-to-session recovery**: harder (higher-volume) sessions need more days after them (tough hypertrophy session ≈ 3 days, easier ≈ 2) [205]; lower per-session volume permits higher frequency at equal weekly work [210–212].
10. **Intensity ceiling**: do not spend more than 8 weeks at 90%+ before a meet, and avoid 90%+ when no meet is near; strength work lives at 75%+ with ~80% being repeatable 2–3×/week [167–169]. Keep assistance work above 60% 1RM or drop it [176].
11. **Proximity to failure**: stay close to but not at failure; 1 rep short every session accumulates fatigue too fast, 8 short never adapts; reference rep maxes ≈5 @85%, ≈10 @75% [132–133, 176–177]. Failure/near-failure is acceptable only in the final accumulation week [133].
12. **Overreaching limits**: a single planned overreach week at the end of the meso is beneficial; overreaching beyond 2–3 weeks risks overtraining; never overreach in week 1 [117–118, 163, 238].
13. **Volume beats intensity as the fatigue driver**: volume is "the single biggest contributor to fatigue"; glycogen fatigue resolves in days, NS fatigue in days to weeks, hormonal/connective-tissue fatigue in weeks [107, 109, 113, 173].
14. **Starting volume when adopting/adapting a program**: start with much lower volume than the source program and ease in; adding volume when recovered is easier and safer than cutting it when beat up [164].
15. **Yearly reset**: 2 weeks (up to 2–4) of active rest at 50%/50% once or twice a year, plus a break from >80% 1RM for at least a month every ~4 months for connective tissue [155, 181, 209].
