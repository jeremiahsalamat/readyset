## Chapter 1 — Important Terms (pp. 12–20)

### Load / effort / volume definitions
- **Intensity ("absolute intensity")**: "the amount of resistance presented during a movement", measured as force, expressed in lb/kg. A 335 lb squat for one easy rep is *higher* intensity than 315 lb x 11.5 reps to failure. [13]
- **Relative Intensity**: "Proximity to concentric muscular failure of any given set." 405x3 with 6 possible is *less* relatively intense than 275x8 with only 9 possible. [13] (This is the book's RIR-type concept in these chapters.)
- **Volume**: total mechanical work (force x distance) over a rep/set/exercise/session/week. Practical proxy: **sets x reps x weight** (distance can be ignored when comparing the same lift of the same lifter). [13]
- **Frequency**: number of training sessions per unit time, usually per week. [13]
- **Exercise Selection**: the actual exercises used (e.g. "squats and stiff legged deadlifts", not just "legs"). [13]
- **Strength**: maximal ability to produce force. [17]
- **Task-Specific Strength**: maximal force in a specific movement, defined by the *weakest point* of the lift ("if you can lock out 800lbs from the knees, but you can only break 600lbs off of the floor; your deadlifting strength is only 600lbs"). [17]

### Session / cycle definitions
- **Training Session**: a single bout; "generally has a warm up, a working phase, and a distinct end which may or may not involve a cool down". Can be multiple per week or per day. [14]
- **Light Session**: intentionally non-overloading, "relatively easy"; purpose is to enhance recovery/adaptation while mitigating loss of technique, size, strength; characterised by reduced volume and/or intensity. [14]
- **Off Day**: no training sessions. [14]
- **Microcycle**: the time between repetitions of a single session type; "typically (but not always) a week in length". Example 4-day microcycle given: Mon bench + accessories, Tue squat + lower accessories, Thu overhead press + accessories, Fri deadlift + lower accessories = 4 workouts + 3 off days. [14]
- **Accumulation Phase**: sequential microcycles in which training gets progressively harder via increased volume, intensity, or both. [15]
- **Deload Phase**: an entire microcycle of light sessions, to reduce fatigue while preserving adaptations. "its reduction of volume and intensity from normal accumulation training must be marked and non-trivial." [15]
- **Mesocycle**: accumulation phase (usually **3–5 weeks**) + deload (usually **about a week**); "usually lasts for about a month". [16]
- **Accumulation:Deload paradigm** ratios: classic **3:1**; **4:1** "common to hypertrophy training"; **3:2** "common to peaking and tapering". [16]
- **Training Block**: 1–3 mesocycles with a unified purpose (e.g. three hypertrophy mesocycles = a hypertrophy block). [16]
- **Macrocycle**: sequence of blocks culminating in competition; typical powerlifting macrocycle = hypertrophy block -> strength block -> peaking block -> meet -> active rest. [16]
- **Mass** phase: high-volume training + hypercaloric diet, 1–3 months. **Maintenance**: stable bodyweight, mostly basic strength and peaking training, at least a month, no upper limit. **Cut**: high-volume training + hypocaloric diet, 1–3 months. [16–17]
- **Active Rest**: almost always one mesocycle; a sequence of specialised deloads; has no accumulation phase. [18]

### Block definitions with numbers (key for the app)
- **Hypertrophy Block**: "higher training volumes and minimum intensities of **60% 1RM**, which translates roughly to reps in the **6-10 range** for most powerlifting applications." [17–18]
- **General Strength Block**: "intensities of **75% 1RM and higher** and sets in the **three to six repetition range**." [18]
- **Peaking Block**: accumulation phase "of around **90% 1RM** lifting", followed by an elongated deload called a **taper** (strength maintained, fatigue reduced). [18]

### Other terms
- **Homeostasis**: stable internal environment; "adaptations are almost exclusive[ly] stimulated by a disruption of homeostasis". [18]
- **Periodization**: logical sequencing of training variables to maximise adaptation, reduce injury, and peak at a chosen time. [19]
- **Flexibility**: range of motion of a joint/series of joints. **Technique**: execution in proper sequence/form; no single ideal technique but large commonalities. **Mobility**: intersection of technique, strength and flexibility. [19–20]

- Seven principles, rank-ordered by importance: **1. Specificity** (sub-principles: Training Modality Compatibility; Directed Adaptation), **2. Overload**, **3. Fatigue Management**, **4. ## Chapter 3 — Specificity (pp. 26–71)

### Definition
- Scientific: training "should tax and stimulate the underlying systems of the sport task". [26]
- Powerlifting: "training that improves the underlying systems of powerlifting performance." Ranges from broad (muscle size) to narrower (general strength of those muscles) to narrowest (execution of the competition lifts). [26]
- 4-layer specificity hierarchy: (1) training the lifts themselves, heavy; (2) size and strength of the muscles needed; (3) tangential (advanced flexibility); (4) so non-specific it harms performance (endurance training). [28]

### Sub-principle: Training Modality Compatibility
- Conflicts, listed from **least** compatible to **most**: Strength & Endurance; Strength & Flexibility; Strength & Power; Strength & Size; General Strength & Specific Strength. [29]

**1. Strength & Endurance** [30–33]
- "Very light cardio sessions aside, serious attempts to improve endurance interfere" with powerlifting. [30]
- Mechanisms: muscle loss (catabolic), non-productive fatigue, fiber-type shift toward slow-twitch (reversible but takes months), nervous-system alterations. [30–32]
- If terribly out of shape, "just doing more volume and higher reps is usually a better choice than endurance training". [30]
- Verdict: endurance training "should likely be minimized if powerlifting results are of utmost importance"; any inclusion is a tradeoff. [33]

**2. Strength & Flexibility** [33–35]
- Three ways to train flexibility: (a) full ROM in the competition lifts; (b) supra-normal ROM lifts (high bar good mornings, cambered bar bench, deficit deadlifts); (c) dedicated stretching. First two have no negative effects; dedicated stretching "can contribute to fatigue and interfere with strength adaptations". [34]
- Stretching causes temporary strength/power decrease (minutes to hours); most interfering right after training, least when done at another time of day. [35]
- Verdict: practice full-ROM powerlifts; use supra-normal ROM lifts regularly; use dedicated stretching only if those fail, "at a time furthest away in the day from the lifts themselves"; "Only get as flexible as needed." [35]

**3. Strength & Power** [35–36]
- True power training optimally loaded "at around **30% 1RM**" — only weakly specific. Maximal intent of movement matters but "the loads used are the same as would be used to enhance strength". [36]
- Power is optimised at low volumes; "Strength training, however, is optimized with moderate volumes ... and size training with high volumes." [36]
- Maximising powerlifting "must jettison power training in large part". [36]

**4. Strength & Size** [37–38]
- Size training is best at high volume, which creates cumulative fatigue; it pays off "in a delayed manner when volumes drop, strength training commences". [37]
- Excess training of non-contributing muscles (biceps, medial delts, calves) adds fatigue "not worth the performance tradeoff"; some is good, and "a minimal amount of balance between the major muscle groups" helps prevent injury. [38]
- "doing hypertrophy training even during strength and peaking phases ... can be somewhat (though in the grand scheme not terribly) harmful". [38]

**5. General Strength & Specific Strength** [38–39]
- Smallest conflict. A lifter can have the best gym 5RM but not the best 1RM; usually technique, which "tends to break down ... at the heaviest loads, nearing 1RM". [39]
- "the best expression of maximal strength occurs in a low-volume environment ... while the best strength training occurs in a moderate-volume environment." [39]

### Sub-principle: Directed Adaptation [40–42]
- Specific stimuli must be presented "sequential[ly] and continuous[ly]" — "if you want to get better at squatting for sets of 5, ... you should probably be squatting for sets of 5 just about every week in sequence, and not every 3rd week". [40]
- Alternating e.g. sets of 5 and sets of 15 each workout means you "won't get nearly as good at sets of 5"; some adaptations are either-or, and training the other range reverses them. [41]
- Programs doing "sets of 5, heavy triples, and drop sets of 20 reps in the same week" are "relegate[d] ... to inferiority" — "jack of all trades, master of none"; gains recede faster when focus later narrows. [41–42]
- Preferred structure: dedicated phases — hypertrophy phase (one to several months), then basic strength phase with "dedicated (**sets of 3-5**) training for up to several months", then peaking. [42]

### Proper application 1 — Lift selection approaching a competition [44–48]
- Competition lifts need not be in every mesocycle (variation), but training must increasingly replicate them as the meet approaches, with most focus during the peaking phase (last mesocycle). [44–45]
- Reasons: muscle/tendon ultrastructure (collagen alignment, pennation angle) becomes lift-specific over weeks; short-term (weeks) optimisation needs the actual lifts, long-term (months) can use variation. [45]
- Assistance work should also become more specific nearer the meet: e.g. skull crushers/overhead extensions far out -> JM presses and shoulder presses near the meet. [45]
- Not performing the lifts "in at least the last several weeks of meet prep" raises risk of connective-tissue injury, lower force production, and technical errors. [47]
- "as the competition nears, it pays to practice as you play". [48]

### Proper application 2 — Assistance work in the grand scheme [48–50]
- All assistance work (outside flexibility, pure technique and injury management) must target only two outcomes: **muscle size** and **movement strength**. [48]
- Size: train the muscles that propel the lifts (chest for bench as a higher priority than rear delts; not calves/medial delts). [48–49]
- Movement strength: prioritise by limiting factor (e.g. back rounds on deadlift but floor speed is fine -> train back, not squat). Limiting factors may exist because they are "already training them too much and not recovering enough". [49]
- Non-directed training to avoid: "Calf, high rep ab, bicep work; Too much lat work (some is likely good); Most types of cardio; Too much yoga, pilates, or mobility work". "if you're not sure why you're doing an exercise, this is not a good sign!" [50]

### Proper application 3 — Technique [50–51]
- Training lifts of the competition movements must be performed exactly like or very close to competition execution. [50]
- Straps on deadlifts and touch-and-go bench "can be OK far out from a meet", but "especially during the last month of training, the training lifts must be performed exactly like the competition lifts". A month out is "the closest to the meet that the transition to pure competition techniques should be made"; better to switch "before the peaking phase begins". [51]
- Programming paused bench sets of 3 off touch-and-go numbers "can mean you're unknowingly programming for sets of one". Transition to paused work can take "several weeks of minute technical adjustments". [51]

### Under-application of specificity [52–58]
- External training interference depends on 4 variables: volume, impact/disruption, modality, timing. [52]
- Volume: tennis 2x/week x 1 h = "minute" interference; 6 days x 2 h = large. [53]
- Disruption: 6 h/week yoga adds little stress; MMA/BJJ is highly disruptive plus minor injuries. [53]
- Timing: place external activity "as far apart from hard powerlifting workouts as realistically possible" (e.g. AM soccer, late PM lifting; sparring on weekends, heaviest lifting Tue–Fri); concentrate external activity in earlier macrocycle phases. [54–55]
- GPP for powerlifting = the hypertrophy phase: muscles used in the powerlifts "trained in a slightly higher rep range (**sets of 6-10 reps**, usually) and with more volume", relying heavily on close derivatives (front squats, close grip benches, deficit deadlifts). Kettlebells, circuits, sled work "don't have much of a place". SPP = "the powerlifts and their close derivatives in the **3-5 repetition range**". [56–57]
- Selection cheat sheet: every method/exercise must unambiguously address at least one of: Size, Strength, Peaking, Technique, Recovery, Adaptation, Injury — all with reference to powerlifting. "KIR (keep it relevant)." If you can't justify an exercise/rep range/set scheme "within a short paragraph pretty much on the spot", leave it out. [57–58]
- Mobility work beyond what the lifts need "does not seem to confer any meaningful advantages". [58]

### Over-application of specificity [58–67]
**1. Training "competition heavy" (sets of 1–3 near PBs) year-round** — four problems: [59]
- **Staleness**: same stimulus for too long yields diminishing returns; "Just training in a different repetition range while keeping all other variables constant can lead to an increased rate of performance gain." Heavy-then-light mixed programs "increase the staleness of both modalities". Dedicated phases of distinct rep ranges minimise staleness. [59–60]
- **Missing hypertrophy**: hypertrophy "is best stimulated with high volumes rather than heavy weights"; "training any heavier than about **80% 1RM** is so fatiguing that the optimal volumes needed for muscle growth are all but unsustainable." [60]
- Heavy work then volume in the same session "to a point": if you prioritise one and do mostly that, fine; a 50/50 split gives "the 'ok' of both worlds rather than the best" — volume fatigue prevents going heavy next session/week and favours repetitive-force neural adaptations. [61–62]
- **Missing general strength**: strength needs "weights that are sufficiently heavy (**75%+ or so 1RM**)" *and* enough volume. "Training in the **3-5 repetition range** (or rather, with weights closer to the **80%1RM** mark than the 90%+ range) tends to promote the best combination of heavy-enough weights and sustainable volumes to promote the fastest strength improvements." "Any amount of needless maximal lifting adds an unnecessary amount extra fatigue". [62]
- **Overvaluing constant readiness**: being weeks from meet-ready year-round "comes at the cost of improving long term performance. You're supposed to be beat up most of the time!" [63]

**2. Very low exercise variation** [63–66]
- Variation for its own effect: after several mesocycles of competition bench, swapping to close grip "can yield more size and strength"; returning to competition bench later "will likely yield higher competition bench press results, especially within several microcycles". "Deletion and replacement" alternation of lifts is "a very productive programming method". [64]
- Directed variation: choose variants to hit weak links (high bar squats for a glute-dominant squatter's quads; closer grip for triceps/lockout; rack lockouts or block pulls for a weak back). Example: quads may need 20,000 kg/week while the lower back tolerates only 15,000 kg/week. [65–66]
- Advanced lifters have clear limiting factors; "intermediates ... still need to develop strengths and beginners ... need to develop everything evenly." [65]
- Doing only the three competition lifts year-round "is not likely the best approach", though correct closer to a meet or for super-elite lifters. [66]

**3. Assistance moves too similar to competition moves** [66–67]
- A variant must "target needed areas, provide a big enough stimulus ... but also, be different enough from the competitive moves". Front squats done like a comp squat (chest tilts, hips rise) give neither benefit; do them "almost completely upright and push with the quads". [67]

### Chapter summary [68–69]
- "growing and strengthening the muscles and movements involved in the sport is the overwhelming priority, with no room for much else." [68]
- Direct specificity (competition lifts, strict technique, heavy) becomes the priority as the meet nears, "with more supportive work like hypertrophy and basic strength assistance work playing an incrementally smaller role." [68]
- "Training foci should be concentrated and specific for a set amount of time, rather than trying to train everything at once". [68]

## Chapter 4 — Overload (pp. 72–103)

### Definition
- A stimulus is an overload when: (a) it "is within the maximal threshold of the adaptive system" (between the minimum stimulus that elicits adaptation and the system's maximal exertion capacity), and (b) it "is on average greater than recent historical stimuli." [72–73]
- "the recommendation to make every session more overloading than the last is imprudent. However, the average session must in fact be greater than the last". [73]
- Powerlifting definition: (a) training must meet "minimum functional intensities and volumes" — heavy and voluminous enough for size, heavy enough with minimal volume for strength, near limits for 1RM technique; (b) "Training must get harder over time" — over weeks/months/years both intensities and volumes (sets x reps x weight) must rise. [73–74]

### Application 1 — Training at Maximum Recoverable Volume (MRV) [76–78]
- **MRV**: "the maximum volume of training a lifter can perform, recover from, and benefit from." [76]
- Five volume zones: (a) too little for meaningful adaptation; (b) some adaptation but not maximal; (c) at MRV — "the most volume that lifter can benefit from"; (d) above MRV but still beneficial, less than maximally; (e) so high that recovery falls behind — net negative. Overload volume = b through d. Goal: "anywhere between b and c, and as close to c as possible" (c–d adds work, fatigue and injury risk for no extra gain). [76–77]
- Finding MRV, method 1 (best): "keep climbing in volume until the next training phase (whether session, microcycle, or mesocycle) is reduced in performance due to high fatigue." Example: adding a set of 10 at 315 each week; the week you fail at 7–8 reps you are "just past your MRV at that weight". [77]
- Method 2: a **fatigue checklist** — "Is the bar feeling disproportionally heavy? Is the desire to train way too low? Is sleep off track and is hunger decreased? If no, then more volume can be experimented with." "performance itself is the best metric." [77]
- MRV depends on intensity: "you can recover from a whole lot more volume at 60% 1RM than you can at 95% 1RM." Also changes with nutrition, sleep, lifestyle — "there's no easy formula". [77]
- "If you chronically under-shoot your MRV in training, you're simply leaving potential PRs right there in the gym... chase your MRV and if you like, stop just short. But don't stop a mile out". [78]

### Application 2 — Hypertrophy overload requirements [78–80]
- Intensity threshold: weights lighter than "around **60% 1RM**" are "largely inappropriate for hypertrophy training". [78]
- Above 60%, volume dominates: "differences in intensity past 60% 1RM (lifting at 65% vs. 85%) have much smaller repercussions on hypertrophy than differences in volume (1 set vs. 4 sets, for example)." Hypertrophy rises with volume until the hypertrophy MRV. [78]
- How much volume: "generally, it's not 3 sets of 5 once a week, but much more than that for most lifters." [79]
- Progression — one of three approaches: (a) volume already high, intensity rising ("adding **5-10lbs** to the bar each week"); (b) volume rising at constant intensity ("adding sets each week to squatting for 10 reps with 315"); (c) both rise slowly ("squatting 315 for **3x10 in week one**, and working up to 335 for **5x10 in week 4**"). "some incremental increase in volume is almost certainly a best practice, and intensity increases likely play beneficial roles as well." [79–80]
- Anti-example (not a hypertrophy progression because volume drops weekly): Week 1: 315 x 5x10; Week 2: 335 x 4x8; Week 3: 365 x 3x5. [80]

### Application 3 — Strength overload requirements [80–82]
- "hypertrophy has a minimal intensity and puts a premium on increases in volume, strength training has a minimal volume but puts a premium on increases in intensity." [80]
- Minimal volume for strength is lower than for hypertrophy because heavier loads are more fatiguing per unit volume, and total carried fatigue must be lower to allow heavy lifting: "you can only be so beat up if you're doing sets of 3 to 5 reps." [80–81]
- Volume anchors: "**one set of 5 per bi-weekly workout is not enough, 5 sets of 5 is very much pushing it in the right directions for most lifters, and 10 sets of 5 is almost certainly too much for most.**" [81]
- Minimum intensity "usually described at around **75% 1RM**"; below that does not signal the nervous system to expand force capacity; "the direct strengthening of existing size only occurs best above 75% 1RM." [81]
- Progression: "volume increases are mandatory and intensity increases are optional in optimized hypertrophy training, the reverse is true for strength training." Volumes "may not need to change much during the mesocycle, but our intensities almost certainly need to go up." [82]
- "Starting a strength mesocycle from **75% 1RM** and moving up towards (and perhaps past) **85%** or your 1RM is likely a very good general approach". Summary: "with moderate volumes, your weights in a strength phase should start out heavy (75% 1RM+), and progressively get heavier". [82]

### Application 4 — Peaking overload requirements [82–84]
- Goals: accustom lifter to loads approaching 1RM; enhance technique for stability at those loads; retain general strength; reduce fatigue while maintaining/expressing fitness in the days and weeks before the meet. [82–83]
- Intensity threshold: "above **85% 1RM**" — only there does maximal-lift instability appear; 3–5 reps at 75–85% allows "leeway with technique and particularly core ... tightness". "There is no way to practice for the maximal lifts other than to actually use near-maximal lifts in training; above 85% 1RM, in the **1-3 repetition range** per set." [83–84]
- Volume: "the lowest of any major phase" — "only a few sets per session of 1-3 reps on the main lifts for most lifters." Reasons: high volume adapts the nervous system to submaximal contractions; fatigue must be very low. [84]
- Progression: volume mostly constant, "intensity will progress in small increments through the phase (until the taper begins)", starting at reasonable poundages and working up to limit weights. [84]

### Application 5 — Psychology of overload [85–86]
- Most powerlifting PRs are **rep PRs, "largely between 3 and 10 reps depending on the phase"**, e.g. "an 8RM that's only 10lbs more than last week". [86]
- "getting psyched up for too many workouts in a row can wear down your psychological drive". Avoidance patterns named as sub-optimal: "unplanned deloads, only lifting when feeling great, cutting off extra volume, or replacing overloading assistance work with easier options". [86]

### Under-application of overload [86–96]
**1. Not enough volume and intensity**
- "It's not good enough just to do a set or two of the main lift per week... Multiple sets and exercise are usually required... If you're doing too few sets, you're actually mostly testing your abilities rather than overloading to improve them." [87]
- 5-3-1 is "excellent ... for beginner lifters", but its low volumes "are simply inappropriate for most intermediate and advanced lifters. Those who have been training for **more than 2-3 years** have likely developed an MRV that's much higher" and should add sets to the structure. [87]
- Westside-derivative low volume (perhaps one to three top sets per week at 1000+ lb equipped intensities) suits equipped lifters; "Raw lifters ... can thus concomitantly recover and benefit from higher volumes. Using equipped powerlifting programs for raw training is thus usually not the best approach." [87–88]
- **Minimum functional intensities** (table as printed): [88]

  | Adaptation | Minimum intensity |
  |---|---|
  | Hypertrophy | 60% 1RM |
  | Basic Strength | 75% 1RM |
  | Peaking | 85% 1RM ("developing technical prowess with near-maximal loads") |

  "Those are average figures, so not every session needs to meet or exceed them, but most should and the average session must." [88]
- Examples of violation: triceps pushdowns x30 at 40% 1RM; strength work mostly at 65% at high velocity; never exceeding 85% in the weeks before a meet. [88–89]
- "True hypertrophy work is very overloading, and looks a heck more like sets of 8 in the squat than it does like sets of 25 in the leg extension." [89]

**2. Excessive speed work** [89–90]
- Velocity is not judged; "Maximal intent to move (MIM)" should be used on "most main training lifts". Speed work "knowingly decreases intensity overload, while MIM does not." [90]
- Speed days helped some lifters only because they lowered volume and intensity, i.e. acted as light sessions. Verdict: do MIM with light-day loads for fatigue management; speed work for its own sake is "almost certainly not worth doing". [90]

**3. Assistance work that violates overload** [91–93]
- Assistance lifts exist "to get you bigger and/or stronger"; rehab/prehab is outside sport training. [91]
- Homeostatic disruption per unit work, most to least: **Barbell > Dumbbell > Cable > Machine**; compound > isolation. [92]
- Prefer the most disruptive movements "so long as you don't exceed your MRV": close-stance high bar squat beats leg extension; wide-grip bench beats pec deck; stiff-legged deadlift beats seated leg curl. [92]
- Exception: when "nearing MRV and still need targeted assistance moves", isolation/less disruptive moves can give better results by not exceeding MRV — "but that's a rare case". [93]
- Unstable surfaces, vibrating/oscillating bars: "a whopper of an overload killer" — no use. [93]

**4. Poor best-effort tracking** [94–96]
- Track volumes (sets, reps) and loads for main *and* assistance lifts; memory errors of 4 vs 5 sets or 350 vs 355 lb break the "greater than recent stimuli" condition; a best 15 lb higher than remembered can waste the next 2–3 workouts. [95]
- Tracking does not mean overloading every non-deload session: injured or near-career-limit lifters may swap a planned overload for a "sporadic deload" on a bad day, or vice versa. [95–96]
- **Autoregulation guidance:** "Training 'by feel' should be reserved for advanced athletes undergoing injury management or attempting to control unexpected fatigue. Most everyone else should stick to the plan." "You might not always 'feel' your best, but most of those times, an overload is quite possible if the rest of your program is reasonably constructed." Most training by feel "results in moderate volumes and intensities at best". "If you can avoid it, don't train by feel, and to the extent that you have to, be honest with yourself." [96]

### Over-application of overload [96–100]
**1. Training to failure too often** [97]
- Advantage: might slightly increase per-session muscle growth (and possibly strength); research unclear.
- Disadvantage 1: "For the same amount of volume, training to failure is incredibly fatiguing" with disproportionate effect on fatigue, reducing the chance of a good next session/next exercise.
- Disadvantage 2: increased injury risk, especially heavy; missing a squat "can be a downright disaster".
- "For the powerlifter, training to failure is of much limited use and does not garner high recommendation." (Possibly useful for bodybuilders with lighter weights/machines.)

**2. Making each workout harder than the next on principle** [98–99]
- "Volume is an especially high contributor to fatigue". "some workouts must be intentionally non-overloading so as fatigue can dissipate."
- Acceptable structures: every other workout lighter/less voluminous (Westside), or intensity rising through the week only if volume falls to counterbalance (undulating, Smolov-type). These satisfy overload "within the timeframe of weeks".
- Making every successive workout harder "will become self-extinguishing and thus unsustainable."

**3. Non-programmed "insanity" workouts** [99–100]
- Examples: multiple high-rep lunge sets, squat drop sets, unplanned maxing, "rep for rep" with a partner, using a stronger partner's weights.
- They can exceed the finite adaptation window and leave the following week's sessions non-overloading, possibly until the next mesocycle. "If you're dying to do one of these workouts, save it for your last workout before a deload." [100]

### Chapter summary / key points [100–101]
- "Lift hard, and over time, lift harder. Make sure your volumes and intensities are within their maximal thresholds, and take baby steps to increase them over time." [100]
- "An often bigger challenge is for powerlifters to resist going too hard at the wrong times". [101]
- Overload can be generated via volume, intensity, frequency, exercise selection, and proximity to muscle failure. [101]
- Train within MRV; adding any modality (e.g. hours of mobility work) costs recovery or other modalities; optimise MRV by "prioritizing highly specific training and minimizing, if not removing, nonspecific training". [101]

## Numbers most relevant to a 4-day, fatigue-averse program (from pp. 12–103 only)

1. Intensity floors by goal — hypertrophy **>= 60% 1RM** (~6–10 reps), basic strength **>= 75% 1RM** (3–6 reps per Ch. 1; 3–5 reps per Ch. 3/4), peaking **> 85% 1RM** (1–3 reps). These are averages: "most [sessions] should and the average session must" meet them. [17–18, 62, 84, 88]
2. Strength sweet spot: 3–5 reps "with weights closer to the 80% 1RM mark than the 90%+ range"; needless maximal lifting "adds an unnecessary amount extra fatigue". [62]
3. Hypertrophy work above ~80% 1RM is "so fatiguing that the optimal volumes needed for muscle growth are all but unsustainable". [60]
4. Strength-phase main-lift volume anchors: 1x5 every two weeks is too little; **5x5 "very much pushing it in the right directions for most lifters"**; 10x5 "almost certainly too much for most". [81]
5. Strength-phase progression: hold volume roughly constant, start ~**75% 1RM** and progress toward/past **85%** over the mesocycle; intensity increase is mandatory, volume increase optional. [82]
6. Hypertrophy-phase progression: volume increase mandatory (add sets or add 5–10 lb/week or both, e.g. 3x10 -> 5x10 over 4 weeks); never a week-over-week volume drop. [79–80]
7. Mesocycle shape: 3–5 weeks accumulation + ~1 week deload; 3:1 classic, 4:1 hypertrophy, 3:2 peaking; deload reduction must be "marked and non-trivial". [15–16]
8. Volume target: approach MRV from below ("as close to c as possible") but do not exceed it; above-MRV adds fatigue and injury risk without extra benefit. [76–77]
9. MRV detection: performance drop in the next session/week at the same load (e.g. failing a prescribed set of 10 at 7–8 reps) = just past MRV; secondary fatigue checklist (bar feels disproportionately heavy, low desire to train, poor sleep, decreased hunger). [77]
10. Training to failure: not recommended for powerlifters — disproportionate fatigue for small extra gain, plus injury risk. [97]
11. Not every session should be an overload; schedule intentionally lighter sessions (alternate heavy/light days, or intensity up only as volume comes down) so that overload holds across weeks. [98]
12. Directed adaptation: keep one rep-range focus for a whole phase (do not mix sets of 5, heavy triples and sets of 20 in one week); phase order hypertrophy -> strength (sets of 3–5) -> peaking. [41–42]
13. Exercise variation: rotate a main lift with a meaningfully different variant (e.g. comp bench <-> close grip) across mesocycles ("deletion and replacement"); variants must be genuinely different in quality from the comp lift. [64, 67]
14. Assistance selection: barbell > dumbbell > cable > machine, compound > isolation; only size or movement-strength purposes for muscles that drive the lifts; drop calf/high-rep ab/bicep/excess lat/cardio/mobility filler. Use isolation only when near MRV and a muscle still needs targeted work. [48–50, 92–93]
15. Autoregulation: stick to the plan unless managing injury or unexpected fatigue; when deviating, substitute a deload-style session rather than grinding, and be honest about laziness vs fatigue. Save any "insanity" session for the last workout before a deload. [95–96, 100]
16. Competition-technique rule: switch to exact competition execution (paused bench, no straps) no later than a month out and preferably before the peaking phase; paused sets of 3 programmed off touch-and-go numbers behave like sets of 1. [51]
17. Intermediate flag: lifters with >2–3 years of training have outgrown 5-3-1-style volumes and should add sets to such structures. [87]
